# Software for multi-omics data integration and analysis: a systematic and critical comparison of frameworks, packages, workflows and platforms, with EasyMultiProfiler as a featured workflow for microbiome research

## Introduction: the multi-omics integration imperative and the software gap

The proliferation of host-microbiome multi-omics studies across nearly every major disease area has made integration a defining methodological challenge of contemporary systems biology. In pulmonary disease, integrative analyses of bulk, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics with peripheral blood profiling have begun to map a gut–immune–lung axis in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and have nominated a four-gene signature centred on CXCL13, IL33, TLR4 and IGF1 [R5]. Cardiovascular research has interrogated six microbiome and eight host blood transcriptomic datasets to identify microbe–metabolite–host gene tripartite associations in atherosclerosis, with experimental validation in vitro, in vivo and in patient plasma, although the authors themselves flag methodological heterogeneity and the lack of consensus signatures across studies [R6]. Neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders are following the same trajectory: integrative 16S, metaproteomic, metabolomic and host proteomic profiling in thirty children with severe autism and matched controls has linked Tyzzerella enrichment and Bifidobacterium- and Klebsiella-derived metaproteins to altered neurotransmitters [R7], while in Alzheimer's disease the combination of plasma proteomics, metabolomics and gut and saliva metagenomics from 87 well-phenotyped patients has yielded composite predictors of dementia severity, including SKAP1, NEFL, homovanillate, glutamate and Paraprevotella clara, validated through artificial-intelligence-based models [R9]. Oncology is not far behind: parallel microbial, transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of tumour and adjacent tissue from 50 glioma patients has connected intratumoral bacteria to synapse-associated host genes and to bacterial-associated metabolites through mediation analysis [R8]. Even within a single condition, the inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome literatures demonstrate that cross-cohort integration of nine metagenomic and four metabolomic datasets [R10], multi-omic profiling of nearly 500 subjects [R11] and longitudinal integration of microbiome, metabolome, epigenome and transcriptome layers [R12] are now considered baseline rather than exceptional.

Yet the operational reality beneath this descriptive success is sobering. The label "multi-omics" is still routinely applied to studies in which each layer is analysed independently and the results tabulated side by side rather than modelled jointly. In several of the examples above, integration is reduced to correlation of pre-computed feature lists, mediation testing of a handful of candidates, or machine-learning classifiers trained on concatenated matrices, none of which formally captures the dependence structures that motivate multi-omics in the first place. The intellectual leap from parallel inspection to joint inference is non-trivial, and the methodological pluralism is large enough that even careful authors describe their own findings as constrained by "methodological heterogeneity across studies" [R6], by inconsistent sample coverage and heterogeneous data formats [R1] and by the absence of consensus signatures [R6]. Reproducibility is correspondingly fragile: re-running a published pipeline against the same inputs frequently yields divergent conclusions because data wrangling, normalisation, transformation and feature-selection choices are buried in bespoke scripts and undocumented parameter settings. The result is a literature in which impressive cohort sizes and sophisticated wet-lab validation sit atop surprisingly fragile computational infrastructure.

This is the software gap that purpose-built integration tooling must address. Single-omics pipelines are exquisitely tuned for their own assays but offer no principled way to harmonise feature spaces, handle missing modalities, align longitudinal sampling or propagate uncertainty across layers. General-purpose frameworks have begun to fill this niche: the Analyst suite provides web-based entry points for single-omics analysis, knowledge-driven network integration and data-driven joint dimensionality reduction across transcriptomics, proteomics and lipidomics [R3], and timeOmics extends the Bioconductor ecosystem with a generic longitudinal framework for multi-modal time-course data, including clustering and time-associated feature identification [R4]. Outside analysis itself, packages such as SUMO explicitly target the benchmarking deficit by simulating multi-omics datasets with controllable latent structure and noise [R2], yet simulation alone cannot substitute for an end-to-end workflow. Within microbiome research specifically, EasyMultiProfiler (EMP) is a particularly explicit recent attempt to close this gap: a workflow that organises microbiome multi-omics analysis into five interconnected modules—data extraction, preparation, support, analysis and visualisation—built on the SummarizedExperiment and MultiAssayExperiment data classes so that storage, transformation and cross-omics queries share a common substrate [R1].

That EMP has to exist at all is, however, itself an indictment of the prior ecosystem. The fact that experienced groups still assemble ad-hoc Python or R scripts for each new microbiome multi-omics project implies that off-the-shelf solutions are not yet perceived as sufficient, and that "integration" remains more a methodological aspiration than a routinised analytical stage. EMP's microbiome focus also marks a clear limitation: although the underlying SummarizedExperiment and MultiAssayExperiment classes are general, the curated knowledge bases, default normalisations and example datasets embedded in the workflow are not, and EMP cannot therefore be claimed to solve multi-omics integration generally. What it does offer is a credible, reproducible workflow for one important domain, and a sharper formulation of the question of how much of that discipline can be generalised beyond it.

## A taxonomy of integration strategies: unsupervised, supervised, and the longitudinal dimension

Integration strategies for multi-omics data are commonly partitioned into three operational levels along the analytical pipeline. Early integration concatenates features from different omics layers into a single matrix that is then fed to a downstream algorithm, thereby allowing models to learn cross-layer interactions at the cost of forcing heterogeneous measurements into a common scale and amplifying batch effects. Intermediate integration projects each omic block into a shared latent space using joint dimensionality reduction — canonical examples include partial least squares variants, multi-omics factor analysis and related matrix-factorisation approaches — which preserves block structure while still enabling co-variation discovery. Late integration analyses each omic layer independently and reconciles the results post hoc, through correlation networks, meta-analysis of feature-level statistics, or hierarchical aggregation of per-block predictions. These categories are useful pedagogically but rarely sufficient as a methodological taxonomy, because many algorithms traverse more than one level or, more troublingly, are mislabelled by their authors.

A second axis cuts orthogonal to the fusion level: whether integration is supervised by an external phenotype (diagnosis, severity, time-to-event) or unsupervised and purely data-driven. Supervised pipelines — exemplified by random-forest or elastic-net models that consume a concatenated or block-penalised feature matrix — optimise prediction but inherit the instabilities of feature selection on high-dimensional multi-omics data, including unstable variable importance and limited biological interpretability. Unsupervised pipelines prioritise structure discovery but leave the user to judge which clusters or latent dimensions are meaningful. In practice the boundary is porous: PLS-discriminant variants are supervised in intent yet perform an operation mathematically close to unsupervised PCA; MOFA-like factor models are unsupervised yet routinely interrogated by regressing latent factors against covariates. The field consequently lacks a clean typology, and it is common to encounter nominally different tools whose statistical backbones are recognisably the same.

The timeOmics framework crystallises several of these tensions. It is described as a generic analytical pipeline — pre-processing, modelling and clustering — for longitudinal multi-omics integration, illustrated on seasonal patterns of mRNA, metabolites, gut taxa and clinical variables in diabetic patients from the integrative Human Microbiome Project [R4]. Its core operation is to fit univariate longitudinal models to each feature, cluster the resulting trajectories using mixture or hierarchical methods, and then summarise each cluster as a pseudo-omic whose behaviour can be tracked over time. This is intermediate integration in spirit: each block is modelled first, then fused at the cluster level. The framework is conceptually appealing because it provides an explicit time axis, yet the clustering step is unsupervised and the cluster definitions are sensitive to modelling choices upstream — a limitation that is rarely quantified in the original report or in its case study [R4]. Claims that the package constitutes a generic solution should therefore be tempered by an awareness that its outputs are conditional on the choice of longitudinal model and distance metric.

The need to accommodate a temporal dimension is not theoretical. The longitudinal IBS study integrated microbiome, metabolome, host epigenome and transcriptome data across multiple time points and identified subtype-specific host–microbe metabolic pathways, including purine metabolism, that would be invisible to a single-snapshot analysis [R12]. Crucially, the same study demonstrated that symptom dynamics, rather than mean abundance, drove many of the integrative findings, meaning that any static integration strategy — early, intermediate or late — would either miss these effects or confound them with baseline composition. timeOmics and similar longitudinal frameworks therefore represent an explicit design choice to put time at the centre, but they do so at the price of increased modelling assumptions, smaller effective sample sizes per time point, and a heavier dependence on the correctness of the underlying mixed models.

The deeper concern is that the proliferation of integration strategies has outpaced the vocabulary used to describe them. Authors frequently advertise methods as "data-driven" or "knowledge-based" without specifying whether the operation performed is concatenation, factorisation, graph fusion or post-hoc meta-analysis, and the gap between marketing and mechanism has real consequences [R4, R12]. Users are easily persuaded that a novel wrapper around an existing multivariate method is a fundamentally new approach, and any systematic comparison of integration software is forced to impose its own operational definitions to make sense of the landscape. Until the community converges on definitions anchored to the algebraic operation performed rather than the biological narrative, the apparent diversity of integration software will continue to obscure a much smaller underlying set of statistical primitives.
