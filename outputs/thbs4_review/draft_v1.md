# Thrombospondin-4 as a skeletal muscle-derived secreted factor: regulatory mechanisms in ageing and metabolism — a critical appraisal

## Introduction: framing THBS4 within the myokine landscape

The contemporary view of skeletal muscle as an endocrine organ rests on the demonstration that contractile fibres release a heterogeneous secretome of peptides, growth factors, cytokines and extracellular vesicles that act in autocrine, paracrine and endocrine fashion to coordinate systemic metabolic, vascular, cognitive and regenerative responses [R55, R58]. This conceptual shift has been driven chiefly by studies on a small set of canonical factors — irisin/FNDC5, BDNF, IL-6, IGF-1, cathepsin B and GDF-15 — whose expression, secretion kinetics, receptor systems and downstream effects in distant target organs have been mapped in considerable molecular and translational detail, including human exercise studies and genetic loss-of-function models [R57, R58]. The same body of work has produced an increasingly refined set of experimental criteria for declaring a protein a "bona fide" myokine, including regulated secretion by muscle cells, detectable circulating concentrations that change with contractile activity, and demonstrable bioactivity at non-muscle target sites [R55]. Few candidates beyond this short list have cleared all of these thresholds. Against this backdrop, matricellular proteins of the thrombospondin family have so far occupied a peripheral position in the myokine catalogue, partly because their canonical biology has been framed within the extracellular matrix (ECM) of connective tissues, vasculature and the neuromuscular junction rather than within the endocrine sphere.

Thrombospondin-4 (THBS4) is the family member with the most pronounced skeletal muscle signature. Originally identified in Xenopus and human tissues, THBS4 transcripts were already noted to be abundant in heart and skeletal muscle, with lower expression in brain, lung and pancreas [R45]. Subsequent work confirmed protein-level expression in developing and adult muscle, including accumulation at the neuromuscular junction, and demonstrated higher THBS4 protein levels associated with the microvasculature of oxidative, slow-twitch fibre beds [R44, R1]. Genetic ablation in mice produces a coherent, if incompletely understood, muscle phenotype: reduced soleus mass and grip strength, altered ECM composition, and — of particular relevance here — decreased lipoprotein lipase activity and very-low-density lipoprotein uptake, directly linking THBS4 to oxidative muscle lipid handling [R1]. The same deficiency sensitises muscle to age-related sarcolemmal destabilisation, while overexpression is protective in dystrophic settings, positioning THBS4 as a structural and signalling component at the muscle cell-surface/ECM interface [R2].

More recent transcriptomic and proteomic surveys have begun to place THBS4 at the interface between muscle and circulation. Six weeks of high-intensity aerobic exercise training in humans modulates THBS4 expression in skeletal muscle, and the gene was subsequently identified as a predicted myokine hub within a secretome-enriched module selectively upregulated after untrained eccentric contractions [R5, R7]. In dystrophic mice, THBS4 appears in serum at elevated concentrations and normalises toward wild-type levels upon utrophin-based intervention, consistent with release from stressed or remodelling muscle [R8]. Perhaps most provocatively for the ageing field, THBS4 is enriched in serum of young compared with old mice, recombinant THBS4 directly promotes dendritic arborisation and synapse formation in cultured neurons, and serum THBS4 concentrations correlate with physical frailty status in older human males [R4, R6]. These findings, considered alongside reviews emphasising THBS4's distinctive pro-angiogenic, anti-fibrotic and synaptogenic properties among thrombospondins [R11, R27], make a prima facie case for re-evaluating THBS4 as a candidate muscle-derived signal in ageing and metabolic biology.

A critical caveat, however, tempers any premature classification of THBS4 as a "true" myokine. The mechanistic evidence for endocrine action remains largely inferential: most experimental data derive from cardiac, fibrotic, vascular and wound-healing contexts in which THBS4 operates as a locally deposited matricellular regulator rather than as a circulating hormone [R11, R27, R45]. Indeed, work in cardiomyocytes demonstrates that transit through the secretory pathway is required for THBS4 to stabilise the sarcolemma, while several of its most robustly described activities — ATF6-mediated adaptive endoplasmic reticulum stress responses, integrin engagement, and modulation of TGF-β/SMAD signalling — are exerted by intracellular or matrix-anchored pools that are not, by definition, endocrine [R2, R12]. The field therefore still lacks the receptor identification, dose-response pharmacology in distant target organs, and conditional myofibre-specific knockout physiology that underpin the canonical myokines, and the predominantly cardiac and tendon-derived literature provides no direct information about how much of the circulating pool originates from skeletal muscle versus heart, vasculature or fibroadipogenic stromal cells. What the current data do NOT yet establish is that muscle-derived THBS4 reaches biologically active concentrations in plasma, engages a defined receptor on non-muscle targets, and mediates an in vivo phenotype that is selectively lost upon THBS4 ablation in myofibres. Until those criteria are met, THBS4 is best regarded as a matricryptic factor with myokine-like associations rather than a ratified member of the skeletal muscle endocrine repertoire — a distinction that frames the experimental questions addressed in the remainder of this review.

## THBS4 at the protein level: domain architecture, intracellular itinerary and secretion

Thrombospondin-4 (THBS4) was originally identified in Xenopus laevis and characterized as a thrombospondin-family member whose predicted sequence bears the structural hallmarks of subgroup B thrombospondins: four type-2 repeats, a series of type-3 repeats (T3Rs), and a C-terminal TSP-C domain, but lacking the type-1 repeats that define THBS1 and THBS2 [R45]. Whereas THBS5/COMP and several other thrombospondins had been shown to assemble as homopentamers, biochemical characterization of TSP-4 purified from bovine tendon confirmed that it too forms homopentameric particles — five globular arms radiating from a central assembly domain with smaller N-terminal domains packed tightly at the core — corroborating the pentameric model derived from recombinant protein [R51]. In equine tendon, TSP-4 additionally forms disulfide-bonded heterooligomers with COMP that resist dissociation under nonreducing conditions [R43]. This is more than biochemical curiosity: heterooligomerization may mask or alter epitopes relevant to immunological detection and therefore complicate interpretation of "circulating TSP-4" measurements, an issue to which the following analysis returns.

The modular architecture of THBS4 is intimately tied to its subcellular itinerary. Domain-dissection experiments in cardiomyocytes showed that full-length THBS4 localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and post-ER vesicles and is actively secreted; that isolated T3R and TSP-C fragments behave similarly; that the laminin-G–like (LamG) domain instead redistributes to the Golgi; and that mutating the major calcium-binding motifs within the T3R causes ER retention and a secretion blockade [R14]. This calcium-dependent step is essentially a quality-control checkpoint for forward transit through the secretory pathway, and it implies that perturbations of ER calcium homeostasis — a feature of dystrophic, ageing and post-exercise muscle — could shift the balance between intracellular and extracellular THBS4 pools in ways that transcriptional readouts do not capture.

Within the ER lumen, THBS4 engages the chaperone BiP/GRP78 and the stress sensor ATF6α. Both the T3R and TSP-C domains are necessary and sufficient for these interactions, and full-length THBS4 overexpression drives the adaptive branch of the unfolded protein response (UPR), promoting the ER expansion and vesicular biogenesis that accompany ATF6α activation [R14, R16]. The original demonstration that THBS4 enhances ATF6-mediated transcription and protects cardiomyocytes from hemodynamic stress was therefore paradigm-shifting: a protein canonically classified as a secreted matricellular component was shown to operate from within the cell as a positive regulator of an adaptive UPR [R16]. This dual biology — intracellular regulator and secreted factor — now sits at the conceptual heart of any "myokine" hypothesis for THBS4.

The dissection of intracellular versus extracellular activities was made rigorous in a transgenic model in which a secretion-defective THBS4 mutant was expressed specifically in cardiomyocytes [R12]. Both wild-type and secretion-defective THBS4 upregulated the adaptive UPR and expanded intracellular ER/vesicular compartments, but only the secretion-competent form — i.e., protein that completed forward transit through the secretory pathway — augmented sarcolemmal stability, while overexpression of the secretion-defective mutant failed to protect and paradoxically worsened cardiomyopathy on an mdx background [R12]. These findings cleanly dissociate two THBS4 activities: ER-resident THBS4 governs adaptive UPR signaling, whereas secreted THBS4 stabilizes the integrin- and dystroglycan-based membrane attachment complexes [R12, R2, R18]. The specificity of this dichotomy is reinforced by the contrasting behavior of paralogs: THBS3 destabilizes the sarcolemma by inhibiting integrin function [R18], and THBS1 instead activates a PERK-ATF4 axis that drives lethal autophagy in cardiac myocytes [R20], underscoring that even closely related matricellular proteins cannot be used to predict THBS4 behavior.

This duality has important, and often elided, implications for how circulating THBS4 should be interpreted, and several caveats deserve to be stated explicitly. First, most serological studies measure total circulating protein and cannot distinguish THBS4 originating from myofibers, intramuscular fibroblasts, endothelial cells, satellite cells, motor neurons, or infiltrating immune cells, all of which express the transcript under stress [R2, R11, R21]. Second, the available data do not establish that THBS4 detected in serum is biologically equivalent to the intracellular pool; a substantial fraction may be ER-retained, misfolded, or in transit, and the appealing interpretation of elevated serum THBS4 as a marker of "impaired forward secretion" remains speculative in the absence of compartment-resolved measurements. Third, because THBS4 secretion is itself buffered by the UPR status of the producing cell, changes in serum levels may reflect altered ER processing capacity, altered cellular source, or altered clearance rather than altered myocellular transcription [R12, R14, R5, R6]. Any claim that circulating THBS4 functions as an endocrine signal must therefore coexist with the possibility that the dominant activity in muscle is the intracellular, UPR-modulating one — and that what ultimately reaches the bloodstream is, in quantitative terms, an overflow whose concentration is a poor proxy for either upstream transcription or local sarcolemmal action.

## Expression and secretion by skeletal muscle: is THBS4 a genuine myokine?

The original identification of THBS4 already established its unusual tissue distribution: very high constitutive expression in heart and skeletal muscle, with substantially lower levels in lung, brain and pancreas [R45]. Within skeletal muscle, THBS4 protein is enriched around microvessels of red, oxidative fibres and is also abundant in tendon [R1], and a substantial pool localises to the neuromuscular junction and to interstitial cells rather than to the myofibre cytoplasm itself [R44]. This pattern — high in muscle tissues but with prominent non-myofibre (interstitial, vascular, tendinous) localisation — is the natural starting point for any rigorous discussion of whether THBS4 qualifies as a genuine myokine, defined here as a factor synthesised by the contractile muscle cell and released into the extracellular space in a regulated fashion to act on local or distant targets.

On the transcriptional side, the case for exercise regulation is moderately strong. Early work showed that six weeks of high-intensity aerobic cycle training modulated a panel of skeletal-muscle ECM genes including THBS4, with the magnitude of change correlating with gains in aerobic fitness [R5]. A more refined analysis of two weeks of downhill versus uphill running — a paradigm that dissociates eccentric from concentric contractions — identified THBS4 as a hub gene within a secretome-enriched co-expression module specifically induced after untrained eccentric exercise [R7]. The contractile-mode specificity, together with its placement inside a predicted secretome module, makes THBS4 one of the more plausible exercise-responsive candidates to emerge from human muscle transcriptomics. It is, however, important to recognise that transcript-level induction does not establish secretion: changes in mRNA abundance predict protein output only loosely across the TSP family, and a coordinated rise in THBS4 transcripts could equally reflect increased ECM deposition as preparation for release.

Circulating THBS4 is readily detectable. In the mdx dystrophic mouse, plasma THBS4 is elevated relative to wild-type and is normalised by transgenic utrophin overexpression, implicating it as a muscle-derived factor in Duchenne muscular dystrophy [R8]. In dystrophic tissue, spatial transcriptomics has localised Thbs4 expression to fibrotic rather than to regenerating regions [R19]. Beyond dystrophy, serum THBS4 is reportedly enriched in young compared with old mice and can promote synaptic protein recruitment when applied to cultured neurons [R4], and in older humans, serum THBS4 tracks with physical performance in a sex-specific manner [R6]. Yet the cellular origin of this circulating pool remains ambiguous: in the adult, THBS4 in skeletal muscle is expressed by interstitial cells and concentrated at the NMJ and around microvessels [R44]; it is also synthesised by cardiac and dermal fibroblasts, vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and astrocytes [R11]. Whether the contractile myofibre itself contributes meaningfully to the serum pool, as opposed to these neighbouring cell types, is unresolved.

The strongest experimental evidence that THBS4 can in principle be released from a muscle cell comes from cardiomyocytes, in which transit through the secretory pathway is required for sarcolemmal protection and from which it is detectably secreted in response to ER stress or mechanical load [R12, R14]. Skeletal-muscle-specific evidence is conspicuously thinner: in myofibres, Thbs4 stabilises membrane attachment complexes via vesicular trafficking of dystroglycan and integrins [R2], and its genetic ablation causes spontaneous dystrophic changes with ageing — but the protective effect has not been uniform, and Thbs4 deletion failed to aggravate disease in β-sarcoglycan- or laminin α2-deficient models [R3]. Moreover, the intracellular itinerary suggests that a sizeable fraction of THBS4 operates from inside the cell in a "matricrine" or intracrine capacity, blurring the boundary between an exported signal and an internally acting chaperone-like factor.

These considerations expose several limitations that argue against an unreserved classification of THBS4 as a myokine. First, the bulk of available localisation data implicates non-contractile cells — interstitial, vascular, fibroblastic and perineuronal — as the dominant physiological source, with myofibres contributing secondarily, if at all, under non-disease conditions. Second, the most reproducible elevations in circulating THBS4 have been observed in disease states (dystrophy, kidney fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension), in which passive leakage from damaged or remodelling tissue cannot easily be distinguished from stimulus-coupled release. Third, and most importantly, the field has not yet applied standard secretomics criteria to THBS4 — namely demonstration of signal-peptide-driven transit through the canonical ER–Golgi route, stimulus-coupled vesicular or exosomal packaging, and quantitative release from healthy, non-permeabilised myofibres under defined contractile or hormonal inputs. Until such experiments are performed, the myokine designation should be treated as a working hypothesis supported by transcriptomic and associative biomarker data, rather than as a settled cell-biological classification.

## Molecular mechanisms: TGF-β/SMAD, hypoxia/HIF-1α, ATF6 and integrin engagement

The clearest evidence for TGF-β1-driven THBS4 induction comes from vascular and dermal contexts rather than from skeletal muscle. In cultured endothelial cells, Thbs4 is rapidly upregulated by TGF-β1 in a SMAD3-dependent fashion, and Thbs4-null mice show reduced tumour angiogenesis [R13]. Parallel work in skin fibroblasts demonstrated that TSP-4 is a TGF-β1 transcriptional target that mediates hypertrophic scar formation via SMAD3 and p38 signalling, with Thbs4 knockdown attenuating TGF-β1-induced proliferation, migration and collagen deposition [R33]. In the pulmonary circulation, THBS4 was upregulated across multiple rat pulmonary hypertension models, with promoter activation driven by SMAD2 and p38 MAPK downstream of TGF-β1 [R9]. These findings establish a coherent SMAD-centric induction module in non-muscle tissues, but whether the same SMAD3/SMAD2 circuitry operates in skeletal myofibres remains untested.

A parallel inducer is hypoxia. In pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells, hypoxia elevates THBS4 via HIF-1α, and HIF-1α binding to the THBS4 promoter was demonstrated in rat hypoxia-SUGEN and monocrotaline models [R9]. This places THBS4 within a small set of ECM glycoproteins that are direct HIF targets, alongside collagen prolyl hydroxylases and lysyl oxidases. The same study reported that THBS4 itself reinforced SMAD2 signalling in a feed-forward loop, amplifying vascular remodelling [R9]. In irreversible pulmonary arterial hypertension associated with congenital heart disease, THBS4 was the top upregulated ECM transcript and promoted PASMC proliferation [R10]. Although contracting skeletal muscle experiences intermittent and sometimes sustained hypoxia, no study has yet shown that HIF-1α directly transactivates THBS4 in myofibres, and the physiological relevance of this axis in vivo remains speculative.

The most distinctive mechanistic axis for THBS4 is its intracellular, secretion-independent engagement of the adaptive unfolded protein response. In cardiomyocytes, Thbs4 localises to the endoplasmic reticulum where the type III repeat (T3R) and TSP-C domains bind BiP and ATF6α, driving an adaptive ER stress programme that expands the ER and protects against pressure overload [R14, R16]. Cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Mll4 reduces H3K4me1 at the Thbs4 locus, diminishes THBS4 expression and sensitises the heart to ER stress-induced dysfunction [R15]. A cardiomyocyte-targeted secretion-defective Thbs4 mutant recapitulated the ER stress and ER expansion phenotypes but failed to augment sarcolemmal stability, demonstrating that the intracellular and extracellular functions are mechanistically dissociable [R12]. Importantly, this ATF6α-BiP-Thbs4 circuit is the only axis with documented operation in striated muscle beyond cardiomyocytes: Thbs4 stabilises the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex at the sarcolemma and its loss causes age-dependent dystrophic changes [R2]. Whether identical ATF6α-BiP coupling occurs in skeletal myofibres, where ATF6α itself regulates exercise adaptations, has not been formally tested, and the molecular bridge between Thbs4 binding at the ER and sarcolemmal integrin complexes remains uncharacterised.

Transcriptional control of THBS4 integrates stress signals through multiple converging inputs. In cardiomyocytes, Krüppel-like factor 6 directly binds the Tsp4 promoter and represses its expression, while Thbs4 in turn restrains fibroblast activation, defining a KLF6-TSP4-fibroblast axis in hypertensive remodelling [R48]. Counterintuitively, cardiomyocyte-specific suppression of TGFβ signalling after myocardial infarction markedly upregulates TSP4 alongside a constellation of protective cardiokines and ER stress effectors, suggesting that loss of tonic TGFβ signalling releases THBS4 from negative feedback rather than repressing it [R22]. Pharmacological lowering of TSP4 by metoprolol in rats with infarction and hypertrophy further indicates that the THBS4 gene is dynamically regulated by adrenergic and mechanical cues [R49], and rapid transcript induction by angiotensin II and arginine-vasopressin within hours of stimulus underscores THBS4 as a classic stress-response gene in the heart [R42]. None of these transcriptional inputs has been validated in skeletal muscle, and the possibility of muscle-specific KLF6 or MLL4 regulation remains entirely open.

Downstream, THBS4 signals through integrin engagement in vascular cells: recombinant THBS4 supports endothelial adhesion, migration and proliferation via integrin α2 and the gabapentin receptor α2δ-1, and Thbs4-/- mice display impaired angiogenesis [R36]. The P387 pro-angiogenic variant enhances macrophage adhesion to atherosclerotic lesions more potently than A387 [R30]. In skeletal muscle, the integrin linkage is most compellingly inferred from the demonstration that Thbs4 stabilises sarcolemmal membrane attachment complexes and protects against contraction-induced injury [R2], although the specific integrin heterodimers involved have not been biochemically defined. The recent finding that Thbs4 deletion did not worsen dystrophy in β-sarcoglycan-deficient or LAMA2-deficient mice [R3] sits uneasily with earlier mdx data and indicates that integrin-dependent sarcolemmal protection is context-dependent rather than universal.

A critical limitation threads through the entire mechanistic literature: nearly all upstream and downstream circuit nodes have been mapped in cardiomyocytes, pulmonary vascular smooth muscle or dermal fibroblasts, and their extrapolation to skeletal muscle is an inference rather than a demonstration. Only the ATF6α-BiP-Thbs4-sarcolemmal axis has skeletal-muscle functional readouts [R2, R12, R14], and even there, mechanistic gaps remain. SMAD3, HIF-1α, KLF6 and MLL4 regulation of THBS4 in myofibres is plausible but unsupported by direct evidence. Equally, the data do not exclude muscle-specific regulators with no cardiac counterpart, which would explain the divergent phenotypes observed when Thbs4 is deleted on different dystrophic backgrounds [R2, R3]. A rigorous skeletal-muscle-specific dissection of these pathways, particularly using conditional alleles combined with exercise or ageing stimuli, is overdue.

## THBS4 in ageing, sarcopenia and exercise adaptation

The most coherent ageing-related phenotype of THBS4 in skeletal muscle is its role as a sarcolemmal-stabilising matricellular factor. In mice, genetic ablation of Thbs4 causes spontaneous dystrophic changes that emerge with ageing and accelerates disease in established dystrophin-glycoprotein complex (DGC)-deficient models, whereas muscle-restricted overexpression of mouse Thbs4 is sufficient to attenuate membrane damage and rescue dystrophic pathology [R2]. These observations place THBS4 within the same conceptual niche as dystrophin and the integrins that it helps to traffic, and suggest that the chronic mechanical and oxidative burden imposed on aged myofibres may gradually unmask a protective reserve normally buffered by THBS4. Consistent with this view, fibrotic remodelling of dystrophic muscle is accompanied by local Thbs4 up-regulation that spatially tracks histopathological alterations, raising the possibility that THBS4 induction is part of a stereotyped injury-response programme rather than a primary driver of sarcopenia [R19]. The corollary is that, in non-DGC models of muscular dystrophy in which sarcolemmal fragility is not the dominant lesion, loss of Thbs4 does not aggravate the phenotype, indicating that the protective effect of THBS4 is conditional rather than universal [R3]. Whether the same conditional logic applies to physiological ageing, in which membrane damage is sub-pathological and develops over decades rather than weeks, remains untested.

A second, mechanistically distinct line of evidence implicates circulating THBS4 in the systemic biology of ageing. Mass-spectrometric profiling of mouse serum has identified THBS4 as a protein enriched in young relative to old blood, and recombinant THBS4 is sufficient to increase dendritic arborisation and excitatory synapse numbers in transdifferentiated human neurons [R4]. The corollary that THBS4 acts as a circulating pro-synaptogenic factor positions it within the muscle-brain axis framework and motivates a narrative in which progressive loss of muscle-derived THBS4 secretion during ageing contributes to cognitive decline alongside, or even upstream of, classical sarcopenia [R57]. This concept is attractive but, as currently formulated, rests on a single young-versus-old serum comparison and on in vitro neuronal assays; neither the magnitude of the circulating deficit in humans nor its causal contribution to age-related neurodegeneration has been quantified, and the muscle source of the serum signal has not been formally demonstrated in old animals.

At the population level, circulating THBS4 has emerged as a candidate biomarker of early frailty in older adults. In a sex-stratified analysis of community-dwelling individuals aged 75 years and over, serum THBS4 concentrations differed significantly between the physically weakest and fittest male participants, whereas no comparable association was detected in females matched for age, body mass index and Fried frailty index [R6]. The finding is consistent with the well-recognised sexual dimorphism of frailty biology and with sex-specific regulation of THBS4 transcription in skeletal muscle, but it should be interpreted with caution. Circulating THBS4 is influenced by adiposity, systemic inflammation and cardiovascular comorbidity, all of which are themselves correlated with frailty status; the published analysis does not formally exclude residual confounding by these factors, and the absence of a signal in females may reflect either a genuine sex difference in muscle secretory output or insufficient statistical power in the smaller female cohort. The biomarker therefore requires prospective validation, body-composition-adjusted modelling and determination of intra-individual stability before any mechanistic inference about muscle ageing can be drawn.

Exercise offers a more tractable experimental setting in which to test whether THBS4 behaves as a regulated myokine. Acute eccentric, but not concentric, contractions drive a secretome-enriched transcriptional module in human skeletal muscle in which THBS4 occupies a predicted hub position, with expression being specifically elevated after the first, untrained eccentric bout and then attenuated upon habituation [R7]. Earlier transcriptomic work had similarly identified THBS4 among the extracellular-matrix genes modulated by six weeks of high-intensity aerobic training in sedentary males [R5]. These data are informative about the responsiveness of the THBS4 locus, but they are exclusively transcriptomic; no study in humans has yet measured THBS4 protein in skeletal-muscle interstitium or in serum across an exercise time-course, and the corresponding rodent data are equally sparse. Consequently, the field lacks a quantitative answer to a fundamental question: does a single bout of eccentric exercise, or sustained aerobic training, actually raise circulating THBS4 concentrations to biologically meaningful levels in older humans, and does such an exercise-induced THBS4 flux recapitulate, in any measure, the synaptic benefits attributed to young serum?

Taken together, the available evidence supports a model in which THBS4 contributes to age-related maintenance of myofibre membrane integrity and, possibly, to peripheral pro-synaptogenic signalling, while its circulating concentration shifts in a manner that correlates with physical function in older men. However, causality has not been established in any of these settings. The genetic evidence in mice comes almost entirely from DGC-deficient backgrounds, and deletion of Thbs4 fails to worsen disease in non-DGC dystrophies, indicating that the protein's protective role is conditional [R2, R3]. Human associations between serum THBS4 and frailty are observational and vulnerable to confounding [R6]. The most pressing experimental priorities are therefore tissue-specific conditional genetics in ageing mice, paired measurement of muscle and serum THBS4 protein in exercise and frailty cohorts, and controlled administration studies to test whether restoring circulating THBS4 in older animals reproduces the synaptic benefits reported for young serum [R4].

## Systemic metabolism, vascular crosstalk and inter-organ signalling

If THBS4 is genuinely secreted by skeletal myofibres, the most parsimonious systemic hypothesis is that it acts in an endocrine or paracrine fashion on neighbouring vessels, distal adipose depots and even the central nervous system, thereby bridging the canonical muscle–vessel, muscle–adipose and muscle–brain axes. The original cloning work placed THBS4 prominently in heart and skeletal muscle, with interstitial-cell expression at the neuromuscular junction and accumulation at motor-endplate regions [R45, R44], and a subsequent study demonstrated that THBS4 protein in red oxidative muscle is enriched at the microvasculature [R1]. These distributions provide an anatomical substrate for a putative myovascular relay, but they also blur the line between "muscle-derived" and "muscle-resident vascular" sources — an ambiguity that complicates any inference about endocrine action.

The strongest mechanistic case for a pro-angiogenic, vessel-directed role of THBS4 derives from loss-of-function and gain-of-function studies using recombinant protein, endothelial cells and in vivo angiogenesis assays. THBS4 promotes endothelial adhesion, migration and proliferation through integrin α2 and the gabapentin receptor α2δ-1, and Thbs4-null mice exhibit impaired angiogenesis in vivo [R36]. This pro-angiogenic activity is amplified by TGF-β1, which upregulates endothelial THBS4 via SMAD3, and THBS4 in turn mediates TGF-β1-induced angiogenesis in vitro and in tumour models [R13]. A translational extension of these findings is provided by studies in which TSP4-overexpressing bone-marrow stromal cells, delivered in a rat focal cerebral ischaemia model, augmented VEGF and angiopoietin-1 expression and promoted blood-vessel regeneration [R31]. Collectively, these data position THBS4 as a vessel-trophic signal, but they leave open whether the muscle-derived pool specifically contributes to the systemic angiogenic response to exercise or ischaemia, and whether α2δ-1, an established drug target of gabapentin and pregabalin, can be co-opted pharmacologically to modulate this axis.

A second facet of THBS4 vascular biology is its influence on inflammatory cell recruitment. The P387 coding variant of THBS4, originally linked to premature coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction [R54, R50], more actively supports macrophage adhesion and accumulation within atherosclerotic lesions than the common A387 variant, without altering overall lesion size in ApoE-/- knock-in mice [R30]. In complementary experiments, Thbs4 ablation reduced macrophage infiltration into both neointima and adipose tissue and prevented injury-induced restenosis in ApoE-knockout mice fed a Western diet [R35]. These observations suggest that THBS4 can act as a matricellular "find-me" signal for monocytes and macrophages and may couple muscle or vascular stress to adipose inflammation. However, the global-knockout design of the latter study, like most of the work cited here, cannot distinguish whether the macrophage phenotype reflects loss of THBS4 supplied by the inflamed vessel itself, by infiltrating leukocytes, or by adjacent skeletal muscle. The apparent discordance between the pro-atherogenic P387 allele data [R30] and the protective effect of germline Thbs4 deletion [R35] further underscores that genotype–phenotype relationships for THBS4 in vascular disease are complex and likely context-dependent.

A third dimension is cardiovascular stress remodelling. Thbs4-null mice subjected to angiotensin II infusion develop exaggerated cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, perivascular fibrosis, aortic dissection and a high incidence of aneurysms, yet preserve endothelial-dependent relaxation in resistance arteries [R34, R32]. In human abdominal aortic aneurysm, THBS4 is upregulated, and lentiviral knockdown of Thbs4 accelerates aneurysm progression in two complementary murine models, supporting the interpretation that the endogenous THBS4 response is vasoprotective [R24]. Together with reports that the THBS4 A387P polymorphism associates with myocardial infarction [R50, R54], a coherent picture emerges in which endogenous THBS4 limits adverse hypertensive and atherogenic remodelling, while the P387 variant tilts the balance toward macrophage-driven inflammation.

A candid appraisal, however, must emphasise the principal limitation of this body of work. Nearly all causal evidence comes from global Thbs4 knockouts, bone-marrow transplant experiments or systemic knockdown, none of which can resolve whether circulating THBS4 of myofibre origin acts on remote vascular or adipose targets. The closest approximation to a direct metabolic-myokine claim is the original observation that Thbs4-null soleus muscle exhibits reduced lipoprotein lipase activity and impaired VLDL uptake, together with smaller muscle mass and weaker grip [R1]; this is, however, an intra-muscular phenotype that does not establish THBS4 as an insulin-sensitising or lipid-handling hormone in the classical myokine sense. A genuine test of the "THBS4-as-myokine" hypothesis will require muscle-specific, inducible loss-of-function and gain-of-function models, combined with measurements of circulating THBS4 levels, receptor engagement on endothelium, adipocytes and neurons, and downstream metabolic flux in vivo. Until such experiments are available, THBS4 should be regarded as a strong candidate inter-organ signal — its vascular, inflammatory and metabolic footprints are well documented — but the case that it functions as a bona fide metabolic myokine analogous to irisin, myostatin or GDF-15 remains unproven.

## Lessons from cardiac, fibrotic and musculoskeletal disease biology

The cardiovascular literature provides the deepest mechanistic foundation for thinking about THBS4 in muscle, and any extrapolation from the heart must begin with the clear conclusion that, in cardiomyocytes and many vascular contexts, THBS4 is overwhelmingly protective. Genetic loss of Thbs4 in mice subjected to transverse aortic constriction produces exaggerated heart-weight gain, increased interstitial collagen deposition, and defective collagen maturation [R39, R53], and Thbs4-null animals exposed to angiotensin II develop worse cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, perivascular fibrosis, aortic dissection, and aneurysmal disease, even while paradoxically preserving resistance-artery endothelial function [R32, R34]. Thbs4-/- hearts also fail to acutely augment contractility or activate the stretch-coupled ERK1/2 and Akt pathways, a deficit rescued by recombinant protein, directly implicating THBS4 in mechano-transduction [R40]. These phenotypes are mechanistically linked to an intracellular itinerary in which the T3R and TSP-C domains engage ATF6α and BiP in the endoplasmic reticulum, driving an adaptive unfolded-protein-response program that is itself transcriptionally sustained by KLF6, MLL4-mediated H3K4 methylation, and—post-infarction—paradoxically upregulated when cardiomyocyte TGF-β signalling is suppressed, suggesting that the matricellular protein integrates opposing inputs to maintain proteostasis [R14, R15, R16, R22, R48]. Consistent with a protective vascular role, THBS4 is induced in endothelial cells by TGF-β1 via SMAD3 and acts as a pro-angiogenic matricellular factor through integrin α2 and the gabapentin receptor α2δ-1, and THBS4 knockdown accelerates abdominal aortic aneurysm progression in ApoE-/- mice [R13, R24, R36].

A critical counterpoint emerges when one moves beyond the heart to organs in which THBS4 is decidedly pro-fibrotic. In chronic kidney disease, serum THBS4 is elevated in patients, its expression rises in fibrotic kidneys and in TGF-β1-stimulated HK2 cells, and THBS4 knockdown attenuates renal fibrosis in the unilateral ureteral obstruction model [R25]. In skin, THBS4 is required for TGF-β1-driven hypertrophic scar formation, acting through Smad3 and p38 in dermal fibroblasts, and recombinant protein promotes cutaneous wound healing by selectively driving fibroblast migration and keratinocyte proliferation [R29, R33]. This dichotomy—protective in the pressure- or stretch-stressed heart, deleterious in fibrotic kidney and scarring skin—implies that the same matricellular cues can have opposing net effects depending on the resident fibroblast phenotype, the local cytokine milieu, and whether the surrounding parenchyma is injured, ischaemic, or undergoing physiological remodelling. The recent identification of a distinct Fibroblast-Thbs4 single-cell cluster that expands in angiotensin II-treated hearts, alongside upregulation of matricellular and oxidative-stress genes in db/db diabetic cardiac fibroblasts, hints that the cellular source of THBS4 may be as important as the stimulus that induces it, and that pro- versus anti-fibrotic interpretations of the same transcript are not always reconcilable [R17, R21].

The musculoskeletal literature reinforces this context-dependence. In skeletal muscle specifically, Thbs4 stabilises the dystrophin-associated sarcolemmal complex, its loss produces age-dependent dystrophic changes, and spatial transcriptomics in Duchenne models mark Thbs4 expression as a fibrotic signature [R2, R19]. Yet deletion of Thbs4 failed to worsen the dystrophic phenotype in two non-DGC models (β-sarcoglycan-deficient LGMD2E and LAMA2-RD), underscoring that the protective function is conditional on the underlying membrane-stability defect [R3]. THBS4 also accumulates at the neuromuscular junction, forms pentameric and COMP-heterooligomeric particles in tendon, and is upregulated in infrapatellar fat pad and synovial fluid of osteoarthritic patients, where it correlates with fibrosis and pain intensity, suggesting a nociceptive-fibrotic axis not yet explored in muscle [R26, R43, R44, R51]. Genetic-association data add a further layer: the coding A387P polymorphism has been linked in replication cohorts to a 2.5- to 3-fold increased odds of myocardial infarction after covariate adjustment, with the proline variant enhancing macrophage adhesion and accumulation in atherosclerotic lesions [R50, R54, R30]. This indicates that even within cardiovascular biology, gain-of-function THBS4 variants can be maladaptive, complicating any blanket "THBS4 is protective" framing. Beta-blockade with metoprolol halves post-infarction and hypertensive TSP-4 expression in rats, suggesting that THBS4 levels may serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker of maladaptive remodelling, not merely a passive correlate [R49].

Several critical limitations constrain the inferences one can draw for skeletal muscle. First, the cardiac literature is dominated by global knockouts and a few cardiomyocyte- or fibroblast-specific perturbations, leaving open which cell of origin—myocyte, fibroblast, endothelial cell, or interstitial stromal cell—accounts for the protective phenotype, and whether the same cell-specific circuitry operates in myofibres. Second, the pro-fibrotic findings in kidney and skin are largely loss-of-function knockdown experiments in injured tissue; whether THBS4 is a necessary driver of fibrosis or an injury-induced amplifier cannot be cleanly disentangled without inducible, tissue-specific ablation. Third, the cardiac data do not establish that circulating THBS4 is functionally important: most phenotypes are recapitulated by recombinant protein applied locally or by conditional alleles in which systemic contributions are minimal, so the leap from "cardiac THBS4 is protective" to "skeletal-muscle-derived THBS4 in plasma is a beneficial myokine" remains inferential. Finally, the molecular switch that converts THBS4 from a beneficial adaptive-response mediator in heart and skeletal-muscle membrane repair into a driver of renal and dermal fibrosis has not been identified, and is arguably the single most important question for any therapeutic effort aimed at this protein.

## Critical perspectives and controversies

The literature on Thrombospondin-4 presents a paradoxically consistent inconsistency: across roughly a decade of work, THBS4 is repeatedly characterised as broadly protective, yet the magnitude, direction and tissue context of that protection vary so dramatically that simple extrapolation between models is not justified. Several specific controversies stand out and merit critical appraisal.

First, the dystrophy literature illustrates the problem sharply. In mdx mice, Thbs4 deletion worsens sarcolemmal instability and accelerates dystrophic pathology, whereas overexpression of mouse Thbs4 or Drosophila Tsp rescues membrane stability in dystrophic models [R2]. The mechanistic explanation offered — selective enhancement of dystrophin-glycoprotein and integrin attachment complex trafficking — is internally coherent. However, a direct test of genetic modifier activity in two non-dystrophin dystrophies failed to reproduce this picture: deletion of Thbs4 did not exacerbate disease in either β-sarcoglycan-null LGMD2E or LAMA2-RD models [R3]. Several interpretations must be considered critically. The simplest is that the modifier effect of THBS4 is contingent on the identity of the primary DGC lesion; loss of dystrophin may create a particular vulnerability that intact sarcoglycans or laminin α2 mask. Alternatively, differing genetic backgrounds, ages at analysis, or muscle groups examined between studies could account for the discrepancy. The data do not, by themselves, discriminate between these possibilities, and importantly, the absence of a worsening phenotype does not establish a neutral role — it merely excludes a non-redundant, rate-limiting contribution in those specific lesions. The therapeutic rationale based on mdx data alone is therefore incomplete.

Second, the cardiac stress literature is similarly bifurcated. In transverse aortic constriction, Thbs4 knockout exaggerates hypertrophy and fibrosis with defective collagen maturation [R39], and the protein is required for acute stretch-mediated augmentation of contractility through ERK1/2 and Akt signalling [R40]. By contrast, in angiotensin II-induced hypertension, Thbs4 deletion yields a strikingly different profile: exaggerated cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and perivascular fibrosis on the one hand [R32], but paradoxically preserved endothelial-dependent relaxation in resistance arteries on the other, accompanied by a high incidence of aortic dissections and aneurysms [R34]. The reasoned synthesis that emerges is that Thbs4 is not uniformly protective but is compartment-specific: its loss destabilises the myocardial remodelling response and, independently, compromises large-vessel structural integrity through an ECM-dependent mechanism. The divergent vascular phenotypes suggest that THBS4's role in the hypertensive vessel wall is at least partially distinct from its cardiomyocyte-autonomous functions, and that failure to disaggregate these compartments has led to oversimplified narratives.

Third, the thrombospondin family itself is internally contradictory in a way that should temper generalisation. Thbs1 overexpression induces lethal cardiac atrophy via PERK-ATF4-regulated autophagy, whereas Thbs2, Thbs3 and Thbs4 do not phenocopy this effect [R20]. Conversely, Thbs3 antithetically promotes sarcolemmal destabilisation by reducing integrin function, a phenotype opposite to Thbs4, and its deletion is protective against cardiomyopathy [R18]. Thus the family cannot be treated as a phenocopy group: even within ostensibly similar matricellular contexts, individual members drive qualitatively different cellular outcomes, likely because their receptor engagement profiles, RGD-domain usage, and intracellular itineraries diverge substantially. Extrapolating Thbs4 function from Thbs1 or Thbs3 literature is therefore mechanistically hazardous.

Fourth, the elevation of circulating THBS4 in dystrophic serum and its normalisation by utrophin overexpression [R8] is often framed as evidence that THBS4 is a myokine causally implicated in disease. Critical scrutiny is warranted. Elevated serum levels may equally represent a passive consequence of myofibre damage, with THBS4 leaking from destabilised membranes or being released during regenerative and fibrotic remodelling. The spatial transcriptomic identification of Thbs4 as a fibrosis-associated transcript in dystrophic muscle [R19] reinforces this concern — the signal may originate predominantly from activated fibro-adipogenic progenitors and interstitial cells rather than from regenerating myofibres per se. Cause-and-effect cannot be inferred from correlation, and the field has not yet deployed inducible, tissue-specific THBS4 deletions in dystrophic backgrounds to discriminate between these alternatives.

Fifth, a fundamental mechanistic ambiguity persists: is THBS4 primarily acting as a secreted ECM modifier, or as an intracellular regulator of ER stress and membrane trafficking? The Brody programme demonstrates that transit through the secretory pathway is required for sarcolemmal stabilisation, but that adaptive ATF6 signalling and ER expansion are exclusively intracellular processes [R12, R14, R16]. This bifurcation has not been resolved in vivo: most genetic models manipulate the entire Thbs4 locus and therefore cannot distinguish between the two pools. The KLF6-dependent transcriptional regulation of Thbs4 in cardiomyocytes [R48] and the MLL4-H3K4me1 axis controlling its induction under pressure overload [R15] add further complexity, suggesting that THBS4 operates within an integrated stress-and-ECM programme rather than as a single discrete effector. Until compartment-specific rescue experiments are performed, the literature will continue to conflate mechanisms that are, at least conceptually, separable.

In synthesis, the field's accumulated data support a model in which THBS4 is broadly beneficial across striated muscle and the vessel wall, but the magnitude of benefit, the cell type responsible, and the dominant mode of action are highly context-dependent. Where consensus is genuinely absent — particularly with respect to the specificity of THBS4's modifier activity across DGC genotypes and to the relative contribution of intracellular versus extracellular pools — the most defensible stance is one of explicit uncertainty, paired with experimental designs capable of finally adjudicating between competing models.

## Open questions and an experimental roadmap

The most consequential unknowns about THBS4 are presently technical rather than conceptual. The field lacks three foundational tools that have been available for canonical myokines for over a decade: validated immunoassays that reliably distinguish the circulating, soluble form of THBS4 from its abundant matrix-bound and intracellular pools; floxed Thbs4 alleles and matched muscle-specific Cre drivers that permit loss-of-function studies without the developmental compensation that confounds existing whole-body knockouts; and a confirmed receptor, or set of receptors, on metabolically relevant target cells. Until these resources are in place, even well-designed physiology experiments will continue to generate correlative rather than mechanistic conclusions. This technical gap explains why authoritative 2025 reviews of the skeletal-muscle endocrine organ still discuss irisin, Metrnl, BDNF, IL-6 and GDF-15 as paradigmatic mediators but omit THBS4 entirely [R57, R58, R59, R60], and why the only human evidence supporting circulating THBS4 as a metabolic signal comes from indirect serological observations in older or dystrophic cohorts rather than from prospective, biopsy-anchored studies [R4, R6, R8].

The first experimental priority is therefore the generation of a conditional Thbs4 allele, ideally a Thbs4^fl/fl line crossed to ACTA1-Cre, MCK-Cre or HSA-CreERT2 drivers, with tamoxifen-inducible options to bypass the developmental adaptation that has made interpretation of constitutive null mice difficult [R1, R2, R37]. Pairing these alleles with compartment-specific secretory reporters — a C-terminal HiBiT knock-in that luminesces only when THBS4 reaches the extracellular space, or TurboID fusions targeted to the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment — would allow simultaneous monitoring of synthesis, trafficking and release in vivo. Such an approach is essential because THBS4 exerts biological effects both as a secreted homopentamer and as an intracellular modulator of the ATF6α arm of the unfolded-protein response [R12, R14], and current literature cannot cleanly separate these modalities. The dramatic phenotypes of T3R- and TSP-C-domain mutants, in which secretion is ablated yet intracellular ER-stress signalling persists, make clear that any myokine claim must rigorously distinguish extracellular from cell-autonomous action [R12, R14, R15, R16].

A second priority is unbiased myofiber-specific secretomics. Conditional alleles should be combined with stable-isotope-labelling proteomics of conditioned media from primary myotubes, with parallel analysis of extracellular vesicles and vesicle-depleted supernatant, since THBS4 is large and hydrophobic and likely partitions unevenly across these compartments. Integrating these data with biopsies from exercised and aged human muscle will require matched assays for both transcript and protein abundance, because transcript-level evidence that Thbs4 is induced by aerobic or eccentric training [R5, R7] has never been complemented by serological validation in the same individuals, and these studies should stratify by sex given the sex-specific biomarker behaviour already reported in older cohorts [R6].

Receptor identification is the third bottleneck. Candidate-based approaches should prioritize integrins, gabapentin receptor α2δ-1 and CD47, all implicated in THBS4 biology in endothelial and mesenchymal contexts [R30, R36], but unbiased platforms — receptor arrays, avidity-based extracellular interaction screening, and CRISPR loss-of-function screens in responsive target cells — should also be deployed. Adipose, hepatic, pancreatic and neuronal cells are obvious starting points given the proposed inter-organ axes [R1, R4, R55]; without a confirmed receptor, claims that muscle-derived THBS4 acts as an endocrine factor remain inferential.

Several orthogonal biological windows already exist and could be exploited immediately. Skeletal and cartilage biology are particularly informative: Thbs4-deficient mice show no overt skeletal phenotype but display transient thinning of articular cartilage and altered chondrocyte matrix production, providing a tractable system for dissecting mechanotransductive versus endocrine roles [R37, R47]. The intervertebral disc offers another window, where THBS4 is downregulated in degeneration and modulates nucleus-pulposus-cell survival through PI3K/AKT [R23]. Skin wound healing provides a third, since recombinant THBS4 accelerates re-epithelialization through β-catenin signalling and alters inflammatory programmes including IL-37 and TSLP [R28, R29]. Cardiac and fibrotic disease biology adds a fourth, where single-cell transcriptomics has resolved a discrete Fibroblast-Thbs4 population contributing to pathological ECM remodelling, and intracellular THBS4 acts through ATF6α to stabilize cardiomyocyte membranes [R12, R14, R21]. These systems already have validated phenotypic readouts, well-characterized matrices and active tool reagents, and mechanistic insight gained in them should translate back to muscle.

Cancer-stromal THBS4 should serve as the human biomarker anchor. THBS4 was the single transcript most strongly correlated with diffuse-type gastric adenocarcinoma in transcriptome-wide profiling, with stromal overexpression defining the matrix signature of this histological subtype [R41], and synovial-fluid THBS4 has been linked to osteoarthritis pain and joint-tissue fibrosis [R26]. If validated serological assays confirm that cancer- and joint-associated THBS4 reaches the circulation at quantitatively meaningful concentrations, the conceptual scaffolding for a muscle-derived endocrine axis will be considerably strengthened.

Finally, paired serum-and-muscle-biopsy human cohorts, stratified by age, frailty, training status and metabolic phenotype, must be assembled. The existing evidence is essentially mono-dimensional: transcript induction in muscle after exercise [R5, R7] or altered serum levels in ageing or dystrophy without concurrent tissue confirmation [R4, R6, R8]. Only studies measuring THBS4 in both compartments of the same individual, ideally before and after an exercise or rehabilitation intervention, can establish whether circulating THBS4 is genuinely muscle-derived.

A critical reading of the present literature tempers optimism. Several ostensibly foundational observations do not survive close scrutiny: while one study concluded that Thbs4 stabilizes sarcolemmal membrane attachment complexes and that its loss worsens dystrophy [R2], an independent investigation found that Thbs4 deletion did not exacerbate muscular dystrophy in either β-sarcoglycan- or laminin α2-deficient mice [R3], indicating that the protective phenotype is highly context-dependent. Likewise, the original characterization of THBS4 as a highly muscle-enriched transcript essential for normal skeletal development [R45] is contradicted by the absence of skeletal or bone-mass phenotypes in null mice [R37]. The data therefore do not yet establish that THBS4 has a unitary, conserved physiological function; they reveal a molecule whose actions are conditional on cellular context, disease state and matrix composition. Any translational claim, particularly in metabolic disease and ageing, must reckon with this context-dependence rather than extrapolate from single models.

## Conclusions

THBS4 emerges from this appraisal as a stress-inducible matricellular glycoprotein with exceptionally deep expression in skeletal muscle, supported by convergent mechanistic, transcriptomic and physiological evidence that together justify treating it as a candidate myokine of substantial interest. Originally identified as a transcript enriched in human heart and skeletal muscle [R45], THBS4 has since been characterised as a pentameric, calcium-binding extracellular matrix protein whose T3R and TSP-C domains mediate interaction with ATF6α and BiP, supporting both adaptive unfolded protein response signalling and transit through the secretory pathway [R14]. The demonstration that flux through the secretory pathway, rather than mere intracellular accumulation, is required for THBS4 to augment sarcolemmal stability in cardiomyocytes reframes earlier dichotomies about intracellular versus extracellular pools [R12] and provides a plausible mechanistic basis for regulated release from stressed myofibres.

A second axis of convergent evidence places THBS4 at the interface between membrane integrity and oxidative metabolism. Loss of Thbs4 destabilises sarcolemmal attachment complexes and elicits spontaneous dystrophic changes with ageing, while transgenic overexpression is protective against muscular dystrophy [R2]. Complementary work shows that Thbs4 deficiency alters soleus extracellular matrix composition, reduces lipoprotein lipase activity and VLDL uptake, and decreases soleus mass and grip strength, linking the protein to oxidative-fibre metabolism [R1]. In the heart, Thbs4 is required for stretch-mediated contractility augmentation and acute activation of ERK1/2 and Akt [R40], and Thbs4-deficient hearts subjected to pressure overload develop exaggerated hypertrophy, fibrosis and defective collagen maturation [R39, R53]. The coupling of membrane stability to lipid handling and contractile reserve in predominantly oxidative tissues aligns THBS4 with the metabolic phenotype most strongly associated with healthy ageing muscle.

A third pillar is provided by human exercise and ageing studies, indicating that THBS4 is coordinately regulated by contractile activity and is detectable in the systemic circulation. Six weeks of high-intensity aerobic training in previously sedentary men modulates THBS4 transcripts alongside other matrix genes whose expression tracks adaptation magnitude [R5]. In an eccentric-versus-concentric training comparison, THBS4 emerged as a predicted myokine hub within a secretome-enriched module specifically induced by unaccustomed eccentric contractions [R7]. In dystrophic mice, serum THBS4 is elevated relative to wild-type and is normalised by utrophin overexpression, indicating its release from stressed muscle [R8]. In humans, circulating THBS4 has been reported to decline with age [R4] and to differ between the physically weakest and fittest older males, supporting its candidacy as a biomarker for early frailty [R6]. These findings intersect directly with current conceptualisations of muscle as a context-dependent endocrine organ whose signals reach brain, adipose, liver and bone [R55, R58, R59].

Several caveats must nevertheless temper any premature elevation of THBS4 to established myokine status. The most recent narrative reviews of muscle-derived endocrine factors, including the muscle-brain axis in sarcopenia [R57], exercise oncology [R56] and broader myokine compendia [R58, R59, R60], continue to catalogue irisin/FNDC5, BDNF, IL-6, IGF-1, cathepsin B, GDF-15 and Metrnl while omitting THBS4 altogether, reflecting the absence of robust muscle-to-target tissue receptor demonstration in mammals. Genetic data are also internally inconsistent: Thbs4 deletion does not exacerbate the dystrophic phenotype in β-sarcoglycan- or laminin α2-deficient mice, in apparent contradiction to findings in other dystrophin-glycoprotein complex models [R3], and Thbs4 knockout paradoxically protects against injury-induced restenosis while aggravating angiotensin II-induced aneurysm formation [R34, R35]. The pro-atherogenic P387 variant further dissociates circulating levels from net vascular benefit [R30, R54]. Most importantly, the claim that THBS4 is secreted by healthy skeletal muscle at physiologically meaningful concentrations rests largely on correlative transcriptomic and serum measurements rather than on calibrated secretomics, and what the data do not yet show is that circulating THBS4 in aged or exercised humans is mechanistically required for any distal tissue response.

On balance, the evidence is sufficient to designate THBS4 as a candidate myokine whose biology intersects stress signalling, oxidative metabolism and ageing-related decline, but not yet sufficient to call it an established metabolic or geroprotective myokine. The next phase of the field must hinge on three priorities. First, conditional skeletal-muscle-specific genetics to disentangle autocrine from systemic actions and resolve the contradictory phenotypes seen in global knockouts. Second, calibrated secretomic and receptor-target mapping in exercised, aged and metabolically compromised human muscle, coupled to sensitive assays that distinguish intracellular retention from genuine endocrine release. Third, well-controlled intervention studies in older and metabolically compromised cohorts, where circulating THBS4 can be related to functional, cognitive and metabolic endpoints with the rigour currently applied to the canonical myokines.
