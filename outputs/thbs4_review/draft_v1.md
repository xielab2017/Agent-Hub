# Thrombospondin-4 (THBS4) as a Skeletal Muscle-Derived Secreted Factor: Regulatory Mechanisms in Ageing and Metabolism

## Introduction: THBS4 at the crossroads of muscle biology, ageing and metabolism

Nearly three decades after the original identification of thrombospondin-4 (THBS4/TSP-4) as a member of the thrombospondin gene family with conspicuously high expression in cardiac and skeletal muscle [R41], this pentameric extracellular glycoprotein has quietly accumulated an unusually broad portfolio of biological functions. THBS4 is now recognized as a matricellular, stress-inducible factor that regulates extracellular matrix (ECM) organization during development, modulates angiogenic and fibrotic responses, and influences the behaviour of cardiomyocytes, neurons, vascular smooth muscle cells and tumour stromal cells [R8, R25]. Unlike its more extensively studied relative thrombospondin-1, THBS4 lacks the classical type 1 repeats and has a distinctive amino-terminal domain with no significant homology to other family members [R41], which may explain why its functional repertoire—promoting rather than inhibiting angiogenesis, attenuating rather than driving fibrosis, and acting from both inside and outside the cell—diverges so sharply from that of other thrombospondins [R8]. Its prominent baseline expression in heart and skeletal muscle, combined with its rapid induction by mechanical and ischaemic stress, has long hinted at a particular importance in striated muscle biology, but its precise role as a secreted signal has only recently begun to be appreciated.

Skeletal muscle is now firmly established as an endocrine and paracrine organ that couples contraction to systemic homeostasis through the release of myokines and exerkines [R54, R60]. The contemporary view is that muscle does not simply emit a list of independent factors but rather transmits an integrated "endocrine code" defined by secretion kinetics, co-released signal combinations and target-tissue receptor landscapes [R54]. Hundreds of putative myokines have been catalogued in proteomic and transcriptomic studies, and the resulting "myokine universe" is invoked to explain the pleiotropic benefits of exercise for cognition, lipid and glucose metabolism, bone health and immune function [R55, R60]. Attention has expanded to the muscle–brain, muscle–bone and muscle–tumour axes, with extracellular vesicles emerging as additional carriers of biologically active cargo [R57, R56]. Against this backdrop, any candidate myokine must satisfy demanding criteria: regulated expression and release by contracting muscle, presence in the circulation at functionally relevant concentrations, and a defined receptor or signalling axis in a distal tissue that reproduces a phenotype of exercise or training [R54].

Ageing and metabolic disease sharpen the interest in THBS4 for several converging reasons. First, the loss of skeletal muscle mass and function with age (sarcopenia) and the parallel decline in muscle quality and metabolic flexibility represent one of the strongest predictors of morbidity and mortality in older adults [R55, R58]. If THBS4 contributes to muscle membrane stability—genetic ablation accelerates dystrophic change with age, while overexpression is protective in mouse models of muscular dystrophy [R2]—then its age-related dysregulation could plausibly sit on the causal pathway to sarcopenia. Second, THBS4 is rapidly induced by cardiac overload and by angiotensin II, and loss-of-function studies in mice consistently reveal maladaptive remodelling, increased fibrosis and, in some settings, aneurysm formation [R30, R32, R35], suggesting that the molecule behaves as a damage-limiting, "rejuvenating" signal whose erosion with age may permit pathological remodelling in both heart and skeletal muscle. Third, TSP-4 deficiency in mice reduces lipoprotein lipase activity and very-low-density lipoprotein uptake in oxidative muscle, producing smaller soleus muscles and reduced grip strength [R1], directly linking THBS4 to the metabolic–contractile axis most vulnerable in ageing and type 2 diabetes.

However, a critical reading of the current literature reveals a notable tension: while THBS4 is a plausible candidate to be classified as a myokine, it has only been nominated retroactively rather than identified through the classical logic of the field. Its strongest claim to myokine status rests on a human transcriptomic network study in which THBS4 emerged as a predicted "hub" within a secretome module upregulated after untrained eccentric exercise [R5]—an inference rather than a direct demonstration of regulated secretion, circulating protein and downstream signalling. Notably, several recent and authoritative reviews of bona fide myokines and inter-organ crosstalk do not mention THBS4 at all [R54, R57, R58, R60], suggesting that the field has not yet adopted this classification. The data do not yet show that THBS4 is secreted from contracting myofibres into the circulation at concentrations sufficient to engage a defined receptor on a target organ, nor that it is packaged into the extracellular vesicles now recognized as major vectors of the myokine code [R54, R57]. Until these criteria are met empirically, THBS4 should be regarded as a credentialed matricellular muscle-enriched factor with strong endocrine potential rather than an established myokine—a distinction that frames the central question addressed in this review.

## THBS4 protein structure, gene family and evolutionary conservation

THBS4 is the fourth member of the vertebrate thrombospondin gene family, a group of five extracellular glycoproteins whose carboxy-terminal halves share substantial homology [R8, R25]. Among these, THBS4 occupies a distinctive position: it assembles as a pentamer, binds calcium and heparin, and is unusually enriched in muscle and tendon tissues across vertebrates [R40, R8]. Recombinant human THBS4 expressed in NIH3T3 fibroblasts or C2C12 myoblasts migrates as a 140 kDa monomer under reducing SDS-PAGE and as a ~550 kDa species in the absence of reductant, and electron microscopy resolves five globular "arms" radiating from a central assembly domain [R40]. This pentameric architecture was confirmed endogenously by rotary-shadowed images of bovine tendon TSP-4, with tightly packed N-terminal domains visible at the particle core [R45]. It is worth noting, however, that essentially all direct visualisation data derive from purified, detergent-soluble preparations; the quaternary state of THBS4 as it sits within the dense, cross-linked tendon or sarcolemmal matrix in vivo has not been captured at the same resolution — the data do not formally exclude higher-order or matrix-anchored assemblies.

The domain organisation of each ~950-residue subunit reflects a thrombospondin sub-grouping that distinguishes THBS4 from TSP-1/2 [R25, R41]. It contains an N-terminal domain unique to the protein, four type-2 (EGF-like) repeats, three type-3 (calcium-binding) repeats and a C-terminal domain, while lacking the type-1 (properdin-like) repeats present in TSP-1/2 [R41, R39]. The type-3 repeats and C-terminal region concentrate acidic residues that confer Ca²⁺-binding behaviour and a low overall pI, with three N-glycosylation sequons mapped in the zebrafish orthologue [R39]. Human, but not Xenopus, THBS4 carries an RGD motif in the third type-3 repeat, identifying it as a likely integrin ligand and foreshadowing the α7β1/αv couplings discussed elsewhere in this review [R40, R41]. Conservation is otherwise striking: zebrafish Thbs4 (949 residues, predicted MW 103,989) shares the canonical N-terminal/EGF-like/calcium-binding/C-terminal architecture with the human protein, and chick osteogenic transcripts encode a clearly orthologous sequence [R39, R46]. This degree of structural conservation across >400 million years of vertebrate evolution provides an immediate interpretive constraint: any phenotype attributed to mammalian THBS4 must, in principle, be reconcilable with the same domain map in fish, amphibians and birds.

A second structural feature of mechanistic relevance, first documented in bovine tendon and replicated in horse, is the formation of disulfide-bonded hetero-oligomers between THBS4 and cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) [R45, R38]. Two-dimensional SDS-PAGE (non-reducing then reducing), immunoprecipitation and MALDI-TOF established that in tendon extracts the two proteins cannot be fully separated under non-reducing conditions, with pentameric TSP-4 contributing both the high-molecular-mass bands and the COMP-immunoreactive species [R38]. This finding raises an underappreciated problem for downstream interpretation: many "THBS4-specific" signals detected in tendon and skeletal muscle by Western blotting, immunostaining or functional assay may actually reflect TSP-4/COMP heterocomplexes, particularly when recombinant overexpression systems lack COMP depletion controls.

The evolutionary picture is reinforced by developmental expression patterns that preserve tissue interfaces across species. In chick embryos, THBS4 transcripts first appear in the mesenchyme surrounding bone anlagen, coinciding with the initial stages of osteogenesis, and are subsequently downregulated — a transient, interface-restricted pattern [R46]. In zebrafish, tsp4b is required for laminin and integrin localization at the myotendinous junction, with the pentamerization domain itself being necessary for muscle attachment [R4]. Both observations place THBS4 at connective-tissue interfaces (bone–tendon, muscle–tendon) where mechanical load is transduced, providing a unifying anatomical context for the broad spectrum of adult knockout phenotypes reported in mouse and other species.

A critical caveat underlies all such phenotype-to-mechanism mapping. Domain-deletion studies of THBS4 — particularly of the T3R (containing the RGD) and TSP-C (C-terminal) regions — are mechanistically privileged because these are precisely the modules that mediate Ca²⁺ binding, integrin engagement, and the intracellular interactions with BiP/ATF6α that have been linked to adaptive ER-stress responses [R11]. Yet because mutations in these domains simultaneously compromise pentamer assembly, heparin binding and/or secretion, dissecting cause from epiphenomenon remains difficult, and most reported structure–function relationships rest on a small number of in vitro cardiomyocyte and biochemical reconstitution systems. The impressive structural conservation across species should therefore be read not as a guarantee of conserved mechanism, but as a strong prior that any THBS4 domain invoked in mammalian muscle biology has a deep evolutionary footprint whose functional relevance demands direct, in vivo interrogation.

## Expression in skeletal muscle: is THBS4 a bona fide myokine?

The operational definition of a "bona fide myokine" advanced in recent endocrine reviews requires that a candidate factor satisfy at least four empirical criteria: it must be (i) expressed in myofibers or closely associated interstitial cells, (ii) secreted in a regulated fashion by physiological stimuli such as contractile activity, (iii) detectable in the systemic circulation under conditions that reflect its tissue of origin, and (iv) capable of exerting bioactivity on distant target tissues through defined receptor pathways [R54, R55, R60]. Applying these criteria to thrombospondin-4 reveals an uneven evidentiary landscape in which some pillars are well-supported while others remain largely inferential.

On the criterion of tissue expression, THBS4 satisfies the basic anatomical requirement. The protein was identified as a thrombospondin family member with high expression in both heart and skeletal muscle [R41], and follow-up work established that in rodent skeletal muscle the protein is deposited preferentially in oxidative, "red" compartments. Frolova and colleagues demonstrated that TSP-4 protein is enriched in the microvasculature of oxidative muscle and at high levels in the medial soleus, with germline loss leading to reduced grip strength and smaller oxidative muscles, consistent with a functional role at this site [R1]. Arber and colleagues reported more than two decades ago that in adult skeletal muscle THBS4 is produced by interstitial cells and accumulates conspicuously at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), where it was originally identified as a denervation-responsive transcript [R3]. The protein's localisation at the myotendinous junction has since been established in vertebrate model systems, where zebrafish Tsp4b is essential for laminin organisation and integrin signalling at the muscle–tendon interface, a function requiring intact pentamerisation [R4]. Importantly, more recent conditional and reporter-based studies have demonstrated that Thbs4 is also expressed by myofibers themselves and stabilises the sarcolemma through enhanced trafficking of dystroglycan and integrin attachment complexes [R2]. The combined anatomical data therefore satisfy criterion (i), with expression demonstrable in myofibers, interstitial cells, and at both major muscle–matrix interfaces.

The case for regulated secretion by contractile activity is, however, considerably weaker. The strongest evidence comes from a human transcriptomic network analysis by Willis and colleagues, who subjected healthy volunteers to eccentric or concentric running and interrogated the muscle transcriptome with weighted gene co-expression analysis. THBS4 emerged as a predicted hub of an eccentric-specific secretome module upregulated after a single bout of untrained eccentric exercise, with elevated muscle expression relative to other human tissues [R5]. This is an attractive and mechanistically plausible finding, since eccentric contractions impose substantial mechanical and sarcolemmal stress, and THBS4 is intimately involved in membrane stabilisation [R2]. Nonetheless, the inference that THBS4 is secreted into the circulation in response to contraction rests on network topology rather than direct measurement of the protein in serum or plasma; whether the transcriptional response translates into a measurable acute or chronic increase in circulating TSP-4 protein in healthy humans remains untested in that study.

Criterion (iii), detection in the circulation, is partly met but largely in non-physiological contexts. THBS4 has been quantified in blood, but predominantly in disease states: serum concentrations are elevated in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension, both idiopathic and congenital-heart-disease associated [R6, R7], and in chronic kidney disease, where they inversely correlate with eGFR [R23]. Gan and colleagues reported that THBS4 protein is enriched in the serum of young mice relative to old mice, an observation that has been mobilised to support its candidacy as a "rejuvenating" circulating factor [R47], and Yang and colleagues detected age- and cognition-related variation in plasma THBS4 in asymptomatic carriers of autosomal-dominant Alzheimer's mutations [R49]. Crucially, however, none of these measurements establish that skeletal muscle is the relevant source, nor that contraction is the relevant stimulus. Circulating THBS4 may originate from vascular smooth muscle, endothelium, or cardiac tissue, all of which abundantly express the gene [R6, R37, R41].

A fourth, often underappreciated obstacle is biochemical: native and recombinant THBS4 is a large pentameric glycoprotein of approximately 550 kDa assembled from 140 kDa subunits, whose proper oligomerisation depends on intact coiled-coil and calcium-binding domains [R40]. The requirement for pentamerisation to support ECM assembly at the myotendinous junction [R4] implies that functional endocrine studies with non-assembled or single-arm fragments risk artefactual loss of activity. This technical bottleneck helps explain why direct demonstrations of systemic bioactivity attributable to skeletal-muscle-derived THBS4 remain scarce.

Taken together, the available evidence positions THBS4 as a strong candidate but not yet a validated myokine. Its expression in myofibers and at the NMJ and myotendinous junction is well documented [R1, R2, R3, R4], and eccentric exercise clearly induces its transcript in human muscle [R5]. What the data do not yet show is that this induction produces a quantitatively meaningful, muscle-source-specific rise in circulating pentameric THBS4 in healthy humans, or that such circulating THBS4 acts on distant organs through defined receptors. Closing this gap will require targeted proteomic assays of serum during controlled exercise protocols, careful source attribution through arteriovenous sampling or tissue-specific secretomics, and in vivo studies using fully assembled recombinant pentamer combined with muscle-specific gain- and loss-of-function models that distinguish endocrine from autocrine and paracrine effects.

## Molecular mechanisms I: From the ECM to integrin signalling and sarcolemmal stability

The classical, "extracellular" view of thrombospondin-4 (THBS4/TSP-4) as a skeletal muscle factor is rooted in its identity as a pentameric, calcium-binding matricellular glycoprotein deposited within the extracellular matrix (ECM) of tendon and skeletal muscle. Recombinant human THBS4 assembles into a 550 kDa multimer composed of 140 kDa subunits when expressed in C2C12 skeletal muscle myoblasts [R40], and the same pentameric species co-purifies from bovine tendon together with cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP), forming disulfide-bonded heterooligomers that resist separation under non-reducing conditions [R38, R45]. Tendon abundance is documented across vertebrates, with high expression in heart and skeletal muscle noted from the original identification of THBS4 as a distinct TSP family member [R41], and evolutionary conservation of the zebrafish orthologue, which encodes a 949-residue secreted protein with the canonical TSP module architecture [R39]. These biochemical and phylogenetic data frame THBS4 as a structural ECM component rather than a freely diffusible soluble factor.

The functional importance of pentamerisation was directly tested in vivo by Subramanian and colleagues, who showed that the zebrafish Tsp4b orthologue is essential for muscle attachment and ECM assembly at the myotendinous junction (MTJ) [R4]. Tsp4b depletion caused muscle detachment upon contraction owing to defects in laminin localisation and reduced integrin signalling at MTJs, and mutation of the oligomerisation domain rendered Tsp4b unable to rescue these defects, demonstrating that pentamerisation is required for productive laminin organisation and downstream integrin engagement [R4]. Injected human TSP4 correctly localised to zebrafish MTJs and rescued the mutant phenotype, establishing that the pentamerisation-dependent ECM-organising function is deeply conserved across vertebrates [R4].

In parallel work in mammals, Frolova and colleagues established the broader ECM context, showing that THBS4 is abundant in tendon and concentrated in oxidative red skeletal muscle such as the medial soleus, where it is associated with the microvasculature [R1]. Thbs4-null mice exhibited abnormally enlarged tendon collagen fibrils, smaller soleus muscles, and reduced grip strength, with no compensatory upregulation of the closely related TSP-3 or TSP-5 [R1]. These data indicate a non-redundant role for THBS4 in tendon collagen fibrillogenesis and oxidative muscle maintenance, consistent with a model in which the pentamer acts as an architectural ECM chaperone that templates collagen and laminin assembly at force-transducing interfaces.

The most explicit mechanistic link between THBS4 and sarcolemmal stability comes from studies of attachment-complex trafficking in myofibers. Loss of Thbs4 in mice produced spontaneous, age-dependent dystrophic changes and accelerated disease in two mouse models of muscular dystrophy, while myofiber-specific overexpression of mouse Thbs4 was protective and mitigated disease [R2]. At the cellular level, THBS4 selectively augmented vesicular trafficking of both dystrophin-glycoprotein and integrin attachment complexes to the sarcolemma, and muscle-specific overexpression of Drosophila Tsp or mouse Thbs4 rescued a Drosophila dystrophy model with increased membrane residence of βPS integrin [R2]. A subsequent study showed that the paralogue THBS3 has the opposite effect, promoting sarcolemmal destabilisation by reducing integrin function, indicating that the balance between TSP family members at the attachment complex is finely tuned [R15]. The convergent picture is that THBS4 promotes the assembly and/or trafficking of a multilayered attachment apparatus that mechanically couples the contractile cytoskeleton to the basal lamina, with integrin complexes and the dystrophin-associated complex as key effectors.

A critical and underappreciated aspect of this extracellular mechanism is that THBS4 must transit through the secretory pathway to exert its canonical sarcolemmal-stabilising effect. Brody and colleagues directly tested this by generating transgenic mice with cardiomyocyte-specific overexpression of a secretion-defective Thbs4 mutant [R9]. Although both wild-type and mutant Thbs4 expanded the ER and activated adaptive ER stress signalling, only the secretion-defective mutant produced sarcolemmal weakness and rupture [R9]. The canonical sarcolemmal-stabilising activity of THBS4 therefore requires productive ER-to-Golgi trafficking and secretion, distinguishing it mechanistically from the intracellular ATF6α-dependent protective pathway that operates through ER-resident Thbs4 [R9, R13]. This distinction matters for the interpretation of loss-of-function phenotypes: a secretion-defective mutant should be regarded as a separation-of-function reagent rather than a simple null, since it engages intracellular stress signalling while losing the ECM-organising activity. The data also imply that the canonical and intracellular pools of THBS4 are not interchangeable in vivo, even though they originate from the same transcript.

A genuinely critical perspective on the universality of the THBS4 protection hypothesis comes from Zarén and colleagues, who explicitly tested whether Thbs4 deletion exacerbates pathology in β-sarcoglycan-deficient (LGMD2E) and laminin α2 chain-deficient (LAMA2-related) mouse models [R24]. In contrast to the worsening reported in dystrophin- and δ-sarcoglycan-deficient mice [R2], loss of THBS4 did not aggravate the dystrophic phenotype in either of these additional genotypes [R24]. This finding carries several implications. The protective effect of THBS4 is not a universal feature of all sarcolemmal and matrix-deficiency disorders but appears contingent on the specific attachment complex whose integrity is compromised, raising the possibility that THBS4's protective activity is rate-limiting only in a subset of dystrophinopathies, perhaps those in which residual attachment-complex trafficking can still be augmented. The data therefore argue against a simplistic "THBS4 as a general sarcolemmal stabiliser" narrative and instead suggest context- and genotype-dependent mechanisms. This interpretation is reinforced by spatial transcriptomic data showing that Thbs4 itself is upregulated in fibrotic areas of Duchenne muscular dystrophy muscle [R16], consistent with its role as a stress- and remodelling-induced factor rather than a constitutive structural component. The available evidence thus supports a model in which THBS4 is a pentameric, ECM-targeted, secretion-dependent enhancer of integrin- and dystrophin-associated attachment complex assembly, but one whose protective benefit must be defined empirically for each dystrophy genotype.

## Molecular mechanisms II: The unconventional intracellular ER stress pathway

The discovery that THBS4 can signal from within the secretory pathway itself, rather than exclusively from the extracellular matrix, represents a fundamental conceptual departure from the classical view of thrombospondins as matricellular effectors. In their landmark study, Lynch and colleagues demonstrated that THBS4 is an ER-resident effector of the adaptive unfolded protein response (UPR): Thbs4 cardiac-specific transgenic mice are protected from myocardial injury, whereas Thbs4-null mice are sensitized to cardiac maladaptation, and THBS4 directly binds the ER lumenal domain of ATF6α to promote its nuclear shuttling [R50]. The loss of protection upon Atf6α deletion establishes ATF6α as an obligate downstream effector and places THBS4 upstream of, and mechanistically inseparable from, the most adaptive arm of the UPR. The accompanying commentary by Doroudgar and colleagues framed this as a paradigm shift, arguing that intracellular thrombospondins must now be considered alongside canonical ER chaperones in models of proteostatic adaptation [R13].

Subsequent domain dissection clarified how a single polypeptide engages the folding machinery of the ER. Using cardiomyocytes and recombinant fragments, Brody and colleagues showed that the type III repeat (T3R) and TSP-C domains of THBS4 are both necessary and sufficient for interactions with BiP (Grp78) and ATF6α, and for triggering the adaptive ER stress signature and expansion of intracellular vesicles [R11]. By contrast, the LamG domain localises to the Golgi, and mutation of the major calcium-binding motifs within T3R causes ER retention and secretion blockade — providing an experimental lever for separating lumenal from extracellular functions. The same group later exploited this lever by generating mice with cardiac-specific overexpression of a secretion-defective THBS4 mutant: the mutant retained full capacity to upregulate the adaptive UPR and expand the ER, yet uniquely produced cardiomyopathy with sarcolemmal rupture [R9]. The implicit conclusion — that lumenal THBS4 signalling and sarcolemmal stabilisation are dissociable — is mechanistically important but also carries an interpretive caveat: it remains formally possible that the mutant protein exerts neomorphic, dominant-negative effects on ER quality control that do not reflect the action of the wild-type protein en route through the secretory pathway.

Transcriptional control upstream of THBS4 has begun to be mapped. Meng and colleagues reported that the histone methyltransferase MLL4 protects against pressure-overload-induced heart failure via H3K4 mono-methylation at the Thbs4 locus; cardiomyocyte-specific Mll4 deletion reduced THBS4 expression, aggravated ER stress and worsened cardiac dysfunction under transverse aortic constriction [R12]. This places THBS4 in a chromatin-regulated adaptive module and provides a direct route through which developmental and stress signals tune the proteostatic capacity of the myocyte. It is worth noting, however, that MLL4 has thousands of genomic targets, and the relative contribution of THBS4 versus other MLL4-regulated effectors to the cardiac phenotype has not been formally quantified, leaving the epigenetic work incomplete.

Equally striking is the emergence of a fibroblast-centred THBS4 module under stress. Single-cell transcriptomics after angiotensin II infusion identified a phenotypically distinct "Fibroblast-Thbs4" cluster that promotes fibrosis [R18], and lineage-tracing studies have placed Thbs4–ATF6α ER-stress adaptation within the terminal transcriptional programme of matrifibrocytes following myocardial infarction [R52]. THBS4 expression in fibroblasts is induced by TGF-β through TbR2/Smad3 signalling [R14], and TGF-β can also act in a reciprocal, myocyte-autonomous manner: selective cardiomyocyte TGF-β suppression after infarction augmented the synthesis of a THBS4-centred cardiokine constellation accompanied by ER stress responses [R19]. Consistent with a broader TGF-β–THBS4 axis, TGF-β1 induces endothelial THBS4 via SMAD3 to mediate angiogenesis [R10]. Together these findings delineate a "Fibroblast-Thbs4 / matrifibrocyte" module in which THBS4 marks and possibly reinforces a TGF-β-driven fibroblast state, raising the possibility that some of the systemic benefits attributed to muscle-derived THBS4 reflect fibroblast output rather than myocyte output.

This intracellular biology carries an unavoidable conceptual cost for the "myokine" framing. If THBS4 acts principally as a cell-autonomous UPR modulator — with its lumenal engagement of BiP and ATF6α preceding, and partly independent of, its release into the extracellular space — then phenotypes observed in germline or tissue-specific Thbs4 manipulations cannot be unambiguously attributed to endocrine signalling. Circulating THBS4 measured in plasma [R47, R49] could in principle derive from multiple tissues, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells and matrifibrocytes, confounding the muscle-as-endocrine-organ narrative. Moreover, the dissociation between intracellular UPR activation and sarcolemmal protection [R9] implies that loss-of-function phenotypes integrate at least two mechanistically distinct activities. Future work will need to deploy secretion-competent versus secretion-defective alleles in skeletal muscle, paired with conditional fibroblast deletion and rigorous plasma-source tracking, before the field can confidently assign metabolic and rejuvenating effects to muscle-secreted THBS4 rather than to its cell-autonomous ER programme.

## Ageing, sarcopenia and the 'rejuvenating factor' hypothesis

The most provocative claim surrounding THBS4 in the ageing literature is that it functions as a circulating "rejuvenating factor" whose decline contributes to the deterioration of both muscle and brain in old age. The cornerstone observation, reported by Gan and colleagues, is that THBS4 protein is enriched in serum from young but not old mice, and that recombinant THBS4 applied directly to transdifferentiated human neurons roughly doubled synapse numbers and increased dendritic arborization [R47]. This work was explicitly framed within the heterochronic parabiosis paradigm, in which shared circulation partly restores synaptic and cognitive markers in aged recipients, and was offered as evidence that a single matricellular protein might be both necessary and sufficient to recapitulate at least some youthful phenotypes. The corollary — that age-related loss of circulating THBS4 contributes to neural, and by extension muscular, senescence — has since become a recurrent motif in reviews of the matricellular and "exerkine" literatures.

In skeletal muscle, an independent body of work converges on the idea that THBS4 preserves tissue integrity specifically under conditions of age-related mechanical stress. Vanhoutte and colleagues showed that loss of Thbs4 in mice produces spontaneous dystrophic changes with ageing and accelerates disease in two muscular dystrophy models, whereas overexpression is protective, acting by enhancing vesicular trafficking of dystrophin-glycoprotein and integrin attachment complexes to the sarcolemma [R2]. The spatial transcriptomic analysis of Duchenne muscular dystrophy by Heezen and colleagues reinforces this picture, mapping Thbs4 expression precisely to areas of fibrotic remodelling in dystrophic muscle [R16]. These findings suggest a model in which THBS4 is simultaneously an ECM-stabilising factor whose protective function becomes limiting in aged myofibres and a stress-responsive gene induced during fibrotic remodelling. The duality is itself a cautionary signal: the same molecule may appear "lost" in some ageing contexts and "elevated" in others, complicating any simple narrative of THBS4 as a uniformly depleted youth factor.

A direct human correlate was reported by Yang and colleagues, who found that lower plasma THBS4 levels were associated with accelerated long-term forgetting in asymptomatic carriers of autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease mutations [R49]. While intriguing, this result carries the correlational caveats that pervade the circulating-biomarker literature: the cohort was small (n = 39), the effect could reflect general health or co-existing vascular pathology rather than a causal contribution to memory consolidation, and there is no demonstration that restoring THBS4 would rescue cognition. Moreover, the mechanistic substrate reported in neuronal assays — direct synaptogenesis and promotion of neuronal migration by astrocyte-derived THBS4 [R34, R47] — is not obviously related to the long-term consolidation deficits measured behaviourally, and the relevant tissue source of circulating THBS4 in humans remains undefined.

A more fundamental critique concerns the rhetorical power of the "young-blood factor" framing itself, which has historically outpaced the underlying biology [R53, R59]. Optimisation theories of ageing, which posit that selection actively shapes lifespan under energy and resource constraints, predict that any single circulating mediator will be embedded in broader trade-offs rather than act as a master regulator [R53]. Error-based and program-based frameworks, by contrast, would predict either stochastic decline of many factors in parallel or coordinated developmental programmes in which "youth" is not a single tunable variable [R59]. Under any of these readings, a single protein whose young-mouse enrichment is causally responsible for multiple facets of aged-tissue rejuvenation is, a priori, implausible. Tellingly, the recent sarcopenia–brain-axis literature is conspicuously silent on THBS4, instead foregrounding established myokines such as irisin/FNDC5, BDNF, cathepsin B, IGF-1, IL-6 and GDF-15 [R58], suggesting that even if muscle-derived THBS4 reaches the circulation, its quantitative contribution to systemic ageing biology remains unestablished.

Finally, the synaptic and sarcopenic axes on which the rejuvenation hypothesis leans appear to be mechanistically separate: the neuronal effects reported in young serum involve direct synaptogenesis and NMDA-receptor recruitment [R47, R34], whereas the muscular effects centre on trafficking of attachment complexes and adaptive ER-stress responses [R2]. There is currently no evidence that the circulating pool of THBS4 declines causally with age in human muscle, no longitudinal study demonstrating that low serum THBS4 predicts frailty or sarcopenia, and no in vivo gain-of-function experiment showing that exogenous THBS4 ameliorates age-related muscle decline. The hypothesis is therefore best viewed as an attractive synthesis awaiting the rigorous longitudinal, tissue-specific and interventional experiments that would test it. Until such data arrive, THBS4 should be regarded as a plausible but unproven node at the intersection of muscle and brain ageing rather than a confirmed youth factor.

## Systemic metabolism and inter-organ crosstalk

The metabolic footprint of THBS4 beyond skeletal muscle is, at present, a footprint inferred predominantly from vascular, adipose and cardiac endpoints rather than from canonical muscle-to-liver or muscle-to-pancreas endocrine circuits. The most directly muscle-localised metabolic evidence is the demonstration, in Thbs4⁻/⁻ mice, that loss of TSP-4 in the medial soleus—an oxidative red muscle with high microvascular TSP-4 expression—is accompanied by decreased lipoprotein lipase activity and reduced very-low-density lipoprotein uptake, together with smaller soleus muscles and diminished grip strength [R1]. Because the readout is intrinsic to the myofibre and its associated microvasculature, this observation is best interpreted as an autocrine/paracrine stromal function: TSP-4 acts as a permissive ECM scaffold that supports oxidative lipid handling at the site of its own deposition. It does not establish that circulating THBS4, released from muscle, regulates lipoprotein clearance at a distant tissue such as liver or adipose, and no inter-organ transfer experiment currently addresses that question.

A second, larger body of work places THBS4 at the interface of vascular inflammation and adipose-tissue macrophage biology. In a Thbs4/ApoE double-knockout model, wire-injury-induced macrophage accumulation in both adipose tissue and neointima is reduced, chemokine production is decreased, vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration are suppressed, and Western-diet-induced restenosis is prevented, with bone-marrow transplantation indicating that stromal rather than haematopoietic THBS4 drives the neointimal response [R31]. Complementary in vitro and in vivo data show that TSP-4 expression is upregulated by pro-inflammatory stimuli in macrophages and biases them toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, with the P387 risk variant enhancing macrophage adhesion and apoptosis [R28]. Together these findings define a vascular-adipose inflammatory axis in which matricellular THBS4 amplifies monocyte recruitment and lesion expansion. However, the same tissues that display the phenotype—vessel wall and adipose stroma—are themselves rich sources of THBS4, so the data do not discriminate between a local matricellular action and a hypothetical endocrine contribution of muscle-derived THBS4. The plasma concentration of THBS4, its circulating half-life, and any muscle-selective contribution to the systemic pool remain essentially uncharacterised.

A particularly coherent strand of evidence links THBS4 to angiotensin II-driven vascular remodelling and aneurysm biology. THBS4 mRNA and TSP4 protein are significantly upregulated in human abdominal aortic aneurysm and in Ang II-infused ApoE⁻/⁻ and TgNOR-1VSMC mice, and lentiviral knockdown accelerates aneurysm progression with increased inflammation and ECM degradation [R21]. Paradoxically, in Thbs4⁻/⁻ mice subjected to Ang II, the knockout phenotype includes exacerbated cardiac hypertrophy, perivascular fibrosis, aortic wall thickening and medial dissections [R30, R32]. That loss-of-function and partial knockdown can both worsen outcome is not necessarily contradictory; it is consistent with a tightly dose-dependent, context-specific matricellular role in which both deficiency and sustained overexpression can be maladaptive, and in which compensation by neighbouring matricellular genes may be incomplete. Additional evidence from a rabbit arterial-to-venous graft model shows that THBS4 regulates VSMC apoptosis through focal-adhesion signalling under conditions of mechanical unloading [R42]. Critically, none of these vascular studies ablates THBS4 selectively in skeletal myocytes, nor do they quantify the relative contributions of vascular versus muscle sources to the perivascular TSP-4 pool. The hypothesis that muscle-derived THBS4 participates in aneurysm or dissection pathogenesis therefore remains plausible but is currently unsupported by direct evidence.

The most explicit cardiac-metabolic link comes from a model of diabetic cardiomyopathy, in which obese db/db mice upregulate THBS4—alongside Fbln2 and Lox—in cardiac fibroblasts in a TGF-β-dependent manner, with fibroblast-specific disruption of TbR2 or Smad3 attenuating fibrosis, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and contractile dysfunction [R14]. This positions THBS4 as a downstream effector of metabolic–fibrotic coupling in the diabetic heart. Yet the study does not interrogate a muscle-to-heart axis: cardiac fibroblasts are themselves a demonstrable source of THBS4, and the relative contribution of any circulating, muscle-derived pool is not measured. The locus is therefore local, not endocrine, and it would be premature to invoke a diabetic-muscle-to-heart THBS4 signal on the basis of these data alone.

THBS4 expression is, however, rapidly and pharmacologically responsive in the heart itself. Left ventricular TSP-4 mRNA is induced within thirty minutes of arginine-vasopressin infusion, peaks at six hours after angiotensin II, rises again at day one post-infarction, and the protein localises to endothelial cells in pressure-overloaded myocardium [R37]. After myocardial infarction, the same group showed a 7.3-fold induction of left ventricular TSP-4 mRNA that was reduced by approximately fifty per cent by the β-blocker metoprolol, in parallel with improved ventricular function and reduced natriuretic peptide expression [R43]. The pharmacological responsiveness—on a timescale of weeks rather than minutes—indicates that THBS4 expression in the remodelling ventricle tracks with, and may be modulated by, the neurohumoral and haemodynamic milieu. This identifies THBS4 as a druggable, stress-responsive transcript, although whether β-blockade alters THBS4 expression in skeletal muscle, or only in the heart and vessel wall, has not been addressed.

A critical limitation unites these findings: the field still lacks a verified muscle-to-liver, muscle-to-pancreas or muscle-to-adipose endocrine loop for THBS4. The single metabolic phenotype measured in a true muscle-cell context—LPL activity and VLDL uptake in the soleus—is local ECM biology rather than a hormone-like effect [R1]. Every other metabolic or inflammatory readout—macrophage recruitment, VSMC behaviour, aneurysm formation, restenosis, diabetic cardiac fibrosis, drug-responsive ventricular expression—is anchored in tissues that abundantly express THBS4 themselves. Tissue-specific ablation of Thbs4 in skeletal myocytes, combined with sensitive plasma assays and target-tissue bioactivity readouts, is therefore the indispensable next step before THBS4 can be claimed as a bona fide myokine with systemic metabolic actions. Until such experiments are performed, the available evidence supports a model in which THBS4 operates predominantly as a locally deposited matricellular regulator, with its metabolic consequences—real as they are—confined to the tissues that produce it.

## Cardiovascular THBS4: a parallel paradigm with implications for muscle ageing

The cardiac literature on THBS4 represents the most thoroughly dissected paradigm for understanding the protein's biology and offers a critical "Rosetta Stone" for interpreting the more nascent skeletal muscle data. Three themes emerge from the heart that likely extrapolate to aged skeletal muscle: the protein's role as a mechano-responsive matricellular signal, the dichotomous nature of its loss-of-function phenotype, and its identity as both an extracellular matrix architect and an intracellular ER stress modulator.

The earliest and most striking cardiac observation is the rapidity of THBS4 induction by mechanical stress. Vasopressin infusion elevates left ventricular TSP-4 mRNA within 30 minutes, while angiotensin II drives peak expression at six hours with protein accumulation persisting for weeks [R37]. This kinetic profile is replicated in myocardial infarction, where TSP-4 mRNA rises approximately seven- to eight-fold and is suppressed by beta-blockade [R43]. In skeletal muscle, where eccentric contractions impose acute mechanical strain on sarcolemmal and myotendinous structures, this promptness is consistent with the transcriptomic appearance of THBS4 as a hub gene following untrained eccentric exercise [R5], positioning THBS4 as a candidate early responder to contractile loading in both striated muscle tissues.

The functional consequence of this induction has been most cleanly dissected in the context of the slow force response. THBS4 is required for stretch-mediated augmentation of contractility and intracellular calcium handling, with hearts lacking TSP4 failing to activate ERK1/2 and Akt upon acute pressure overload and developing greater chamber dilation, formally placing THBS4 in the mechano-transductive axis linking wall stress to contractile reserve [R36]. This is the conceptual foundation for interpreting the dystrophic phenotype of Thbs4-/- skeletal muscle: if THBS4 is required for stress-induced trafficking of integrin and dystroglycan attachment complexes in cardiomyocytes [R9], the same mechanism plausibly explains why Thbs4-/- myofibres exhibit spontaneous dystrophic changes with age and accelerated disease in mdx and δ-sarcoglycan-null mice [R2].

However, the cardiac literature also delivers a sobering lesson on context-dependence. In contrast to its protective role in acute mechano-adaptation, loss of THBS4 produces exaggerated maladaptive remodelling under chronic pressure overload. Thbs4-/- mice subjected to transverse aortic constriction develop greater heart weight and increased interstitial collagen deposition, with defective collagen maturation identified as the primary driver [R35]. This is reinforced by evidence that TSP-4 null hearts exhibit pronounced hypertrophy and fibrosis with abnormal collagen maturation in response to pressure overload [R44]. Angiotensin II-treated Thbs4-/- mice develop exacerbated cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, perivascular fibrosis and frank aortic dissection, despite preserved endothelial-dependent relaxation in resistance arteries [R30]. The protein is therefore neither uniformly protective nor uniformly deleterious: it is essential for adaptive mechano-transduction but dispensable for, or even antagonistic to, the fibrotic response to sustained neurohumoral stress.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of the angiotensin II-stressed heart identified a phenotypically distinct fibroblast population, Fibroblast-Thbs4, that emerges as a discrete cell state driving pathological remodelling [R18]. This is echoed by the recent reframing of late scar maturation as a terminal "matrifibrocyte" state, in which fibroblasts adopt a cartilage-like ECM programme that includes THBS4 alongside Comp, Chad and Sfrp2, with Thbs4–Atf6α ER-stress adaptation proposed as part of the terminal transcriptional module [R52]. Analogous Fibroblast-Thbs4 or matrifibrocyte-like states have not yet been identified in skeletal muscle, but spatial transcriptomic data in Duchenne muscular dystrophy show Thbs4 expression concentrated in fibrotic foci [R16], raising the hypothesis that a comparable fibroblast state contributes to the fibroadipogenic infiltrate of aged and dystrophic muscle.

The transcriptional control of cardiac THBS4 is also informative. The histone H3K4 mono-methyltransferase MLL4 transcriptionally activates Thbs4 in cardiomyocytes, and Mll4 deletion exacerbates ER stress and pressure-overload-induced heart failure [R12]. This converges with the intracellular function of THBS4 in potentiating the ATF6α adaptive ER stress programme [R13, R50], providing a coherent molecular axis from chromatin to unfolded protein response. The T3R and TSP-C domains mediate interactions with the ER chaperone BiP and ATF6α [R11], and the same pathway is likely operant in skeletal muscle, where ageing imposes chronic ER stress and where Thbs4 ablation has been shown to reduce soleus mass and grip strength [R1].

Beyond structural remodelling, THBS4 has direct electrophysiological consequences. Exogenous TSP-4 prolongs action potential duration in rat ventricular myocytes by suppressing Kv and L-type Ca²⁺ currents independently of channel transcription [R26]. Whether a parallel mechanism operates at the sarcolemma or transverse tubules of skeletal muscle is unknown, but the principle that a matricellular protein can remodel excitation-contraction coupling is an important precedent that should be tested in aged myofibres, where excitability and calcium handling are well documented to decline.

In pulmonary hypertension, two independent studies show that THBS4 is upregulated in remodelled pulmonary arteries and in patient serum, driven by HIF-1α under hypoxia and by TGF-β1/SMAD2/p38 signalling in smooth muscle cells [R6, R7]. TGF-β is also a major driver of THBS4 induction in db/db diabetic cardiomyopathy [R14] and in endothelial cells [R10], and bioinformatic analysis of dilated cardiomyopathy places THBS4 among ER-stress-related hub genes influencing macrophage differentiation and apoptosis [R48]. TGF-β thus emerges as a near-universal upstream regulator of THBS4 across cardiovascular contexts, paralleling its role as a driver of fibrotic reprogramming in skeletal muscle.

A critical limitation of this extrapolation must be stated explicitly. The cardiac protective-versus-deleterious dichotomy rests largely on whole-organism knockouts subject to discrete stressors, in which the cell of action is often ambiguous: cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and infiltrating macrophages all express or respond to THBS4. The finding that THBS4 ablation reduces neointimal macrophage accumulation and restenosis [R31], and that recombinant TSP-4 can drive macrophage pro-inflammatory polarisation [R28], illustrates that loss-of-function data are not univocally "protective". None of the cited studies formally dissects the contribution of intracellular versus extracellular THBS4 pools in vivo, and the claim that ER-resident THBS4 is the principal effector of the ATF6α programme [R11, R13, R50] remains largely inferred from in vitro domain mutagenesis rather than conditional genetic dissection. Equally important, the failure of THBS4 deletion to worsen muscular dystrophy in β-sarcoglycan- and laminin α2-deficient backgrounds [R24] warns that the cardiac "protective" phenotype cannot be assumed to generalise across all genetic contexts of striated muscle disease. The duality of cardiac THBS4 phenotypes is therefore genuine and likely extends to aged skeletal muscle, but its mechanistic basis—particularly the relative contributions of cell-autonomous ER stress modulation versus ECM-dependent intercellular signalling versus intrinsic electrophysiological effects—remains incompletely resolved.

## Critical perspectives and controversies

The most striking feature of the THBS4 literature is its context-dependence: the same molecule is reported as protective, neutral or deleterious depending on the genetic, mechanical and inflammatory backdrop. Several explicit paradoxes challenge the simple framing of THBS4 as a "rejuvenating myokine" and demand a more nuanced working model.

The muscular dystrophy literature provides the clearest illustration. THBS4 overexpression or genetic sufficiency mitigates disease in dystrophin- and δ-sarcoglycan-deficient models, and Thbs4 ablation worsens the dystrophic phenotype by destabilising attachment-complex trafficking at the sarcolemma. Yet the same study from Zarén and colleagues shows that Thbs4 deletion does not aggravate disease in β-sarcoglycan- or laminin-α2-deficient mice [R24]. This is not a trivial negative finding: it implies that THBS4's protective action is restricted to specific lesion sites within the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex, rather than acting as a universal sarcolemmal stabiliser. One plausible interpretation is that THBS4's pentameric C-terminal domain must engage a still-intact subcomplex to nucleate compensatory adhesion; once β-sarcoglycan or laminin-α2 are missing, the scaffold on which THBS4 acts is gone. A critical caveat is warranted: these experiments were performed in a single laboratory on one genetic background and have not yet been independently replicated, so the negative result should not be over-interpreted as definitive evidence of pathway irrelevance.

A second paradox concerns family-member specificity. Thbs4-null mice develop exaggerated hypertrophy and fibrosis after transverse aortic constriction, supporting an adaptive role for THBS4 in pressure overload [R35]. However, the parallel study from Vanhoutte and colleagues demonstrates that the atrophy-inducing activity within the thrombospondin family resides in Thbs1, not Thbs2, Thbs3 or Thbs4 [R17]. Mechanistically, Thbs1 engages PERK–ATF4 to drive lethal autophagy, whereas THBS4 preferentially signals through the ATF6α arm of the unfolded-protein response [R50]. The two thrombospondins therefore occupy distinct, even antagonistic, signalling niches despite their structural homology, and inferences drawn from whole-family overexpression studies should be treated with caution.

Cardiovascular work generates yet another apparent contradiction. Global Thbs4 knockout on an angiotensin II hypertension background produces exaggerated cardiac hypertrophy, perivascular fibrosis, aortic wall thickening, medial dissections and a high incidence of aortic aneurysms [R32, R30]. Yet the same loss of Thbs4 in an angiotensin II-induced abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) model is vasoprotective, because here TSP-4 is induced as an adaptive response and its knockdown accelerates aneurysm progression with increased inflammation [R21]. The reconciliation lies in the dominant mechanical and inflammatory context: in hypertensive small vessels and the ascending aorta, THBS4 contributes to load-bearing matrix maturation, and its absence de-stabilises the wall; in AAA, where inflammation dominates, THBS4's anti-inflammatory and matrix-protective effects become dominant. This duality is therapeutically important, because systemic THBS4 supplementation could be catastrophic in hypertensive patients but beneficial in established aneurysm.

The fibrotic literature is similarly dichotomous. In the heart, THBS4 is anti-fibrotic, with its loss producing excessive collagen deposition after pressure overload [R35], and the Thbs4–ATF6α axis is even proposed as a defining feature of the adaptive matrifibrocyte programme [R52]. In stark contrast, THBS4 is pro-fibrotic in chronic kidney disease, where serum levels rise with declining eGFR and knockdown attenuates fibrosis in the UUO model [R23], and in preeclampsia-associated trophoblast dysfunction, where reduced THBS4 inhibits TGF-β1/Smad-driven invasion [R22]. In the tumour microenvironment, fibroblastic mutant p53 drives THBS4 secretion that fuels PI3K/AKT signalling and HER2-driven mammary tumour progression [R51]. These findings are mechanistically coherent if one accepts that THBS4 amplifies whatever TGF-β1/Smad or PI3K/AKT programme is already running in the local niche: anti-fibrotic when matrix is being actively remodelled and inflammation is contained, pro-fibrotic when TGF-β tone is high and stromal cells are primed for deposition. The PI3K/AKT-mediated anti-apoptotic effects described in LPS-stimulated nucleus pulposus cells [R20] and in HER2+ mammary cancer [R51] reinforce this view: THBS4 reads the local kinase landscape and potentiates survival signalling wherever it engages.

The cancer literature compounds these concerns. THBS4 is elevated in hepatocellular carcinoma and independently predicts shorter survival [R29]; in colorectal cancer it acts downstream of PDGFRβ to drive invasion [R27]; and THBS4 polymorphisms show modest associations with gastric cancer risk and prognosis [R33]. Any therapeutic strategy aimed at boosting circulating THBS4 for muscle or cognitive benefit must therefore contend with its tumour-promoting potential, a tension that is rarely acknowledged in the "rejuvenating factor" narrative.

Finally, the central nervous system literature adds further nuance. Thbs4 deficiency impairs neuronal migration in the subventricular zone and rostral migratory stream [R34], yet recombinant THBS4 promotes synapse formation when applied with serum from young mice [R47] and lower plasma THBS4 associates with accelerated long-term forgetting in pre-symptomatic carriers of familial Alzheimer's mutations [R49]. These findings point to age- and region-specific roles that are unlikely to be captured by systemic dosing.

Synthesising these observations, I propose that THBS4 functions as a condition-dependent matricellular buffer, whose net phenotypic effect is set by the underlying mechanical load, the inflammatory milieu, the available integrin/receptor repertoire and the pre-existing ER-stress and kinase landscape. Within this framework the apparent contradictions largely resolve, but they also impose strict limits on any therapeutic extrapolation. A critical limitation of the field is that most causal inferences derive from whole-body knockouts that conflate muscle, vascular, stromal and immune compartments; cell-type-resolved conditional genetics, longitudinal circulating-THBS4 measurements in well-phenotyped human cohorts, and rigorous dose-finding studies will be required before the "THBS4 as systemic rejuvenating factor" hypothesis can move from bench to bedside.

## Open questions and experimental roadmap

The transition of THBS4 from a candidate matricellular protein to a credible endocrine signal hinges on resolving a small but consequential set of experimental priorities. Three gaps dominate the current landscape: we lack quantitative, isoform-resolved measurements of THBS4 in human circulation across the variables that define ageing and metabolic disease; the genetic tools available—almost exclusively constitutive germline knockouts [R1, R30, R31, R32]—cannot disentangle muscle-autonomous from systemic actions; and the field has not yet defined the dominant mode by which THBS4 reaches distant tissues, complicating any claim of bona fide myokine status [R54]. Addressing these gaps in a coordinated manner is a prerequisite for translating the suggestive network-level predictions of THBS4 as a contraction-induced secretome hub [R5] into actionable biology.

A first experimental priority is the deployment of validated sandwich ELISAs for circulating THBS4 in deeply phenotyped human cohorts stratified by age, sex, training status, and metabolic disease. The available serological data are fragmentary: THBS4 protein can be detected in serum from young but not old mice, where it promotes synaptogenesis [R47], and plasma THBS4 levels have been linked to cognitive trajectories in pre-symptomatic carriers of autosomal dominant Alzheimer's mutations [R49], but no systematic cross-sectional atlas exists for human skeletal muscle ageing. Because THBS4 circulates as a disulfide-bonded pentamer [R40, R45] and can form heterooligomers with cartilage oligomeric matrix protein [R38], antibody pairs must be benchmarked against recombinant pentameric standards and validated for specificity across splice isoforms; the field's reliance on commercial kits whose epitope coverage is rarely disclosed is an underappreciated source of irreproducibility. Correlating these measurements with muscle mass, strength, insulin sensitivity, and habitual physical activity would test the most basic prediction of the "rejuvenating factor" hypothesis—that circulating THBS4 tracks the integrated metabolic health of skeletal muscle.

Second, the field requires muscle-specific and inducible Thbs4 conditional alleles. Virtually all mechanistic insights derive from constitutive null mice [R1, R30, R31, R32] or from cardiac-specific transgenics driven by strong promoters that do not recapitulate the modest, context-dependent induction observed in exercised human muscle [R5]. These models cannot distinguish whether THBS4 acts locally within the muscle niche—where it stabilizes the sarcolemma and supports integrin signaling at the myotendinous junction [R2, R4]—or whether it mediates true endocrine effects. An inducible, muscle-restricted Cre driver crossed to a floxed Thbs4 allele, complemented by a knock-in allowing near-endogenous Cre-dependent overexpression, would mirror physiological induction more faithfully than existing transgenics. Such tools are essential because the intracellular, ER-stress-mediated arm of THBS4 signaling [R11, R50] may produce dominant cell-autonomous phenotypes that mask any endocrine readout in conventional knockouts.

Third, gain-of-function experiments must be repeated with authentically pentameric recombinant THBS4. The pentamerization domain is required for laminin localization and integrin engagement at myotendinous junctions in zebrafish [R4], and recombinant human THBS4 assembles into a 550 kDa multimer [R40]. Yet most published in vitro studies use monomeric fragments, truncation mutants, or undefined oligomeric states, rendering the literature difficult to integrate. Producing native pentamer under GMP-compatible conditions—verified by mass photometry and electron microscopy—and administering it in vivo at concentrations matching the physiological range observed in young serum [R47] is the only credible path to bona fide endocrine gain-of-function.

A fourth priority is to define whether THBS4 acts predominantly as a soluble factor or as an extracellular-vesicle cargo. Recent reviews of the bone–muscle unit have emphasized EVs as central mediators of inter-organ communication in physiology and ageing [R57], and the broader myokine literature increasingly recognizes that exerkines are delivered in distinct vesicular and non-vesicular pools with different target-tissue receptor landscapes [R54, R55, R60]. Whether circulating THBS4 partitions into one compartment or the other—and whether this partition shifts with age, exercise, or disease—remains untested, and disambiguation requires density-gradient EV isolation with immune-depletion strategies anchored by muscle-restricted tagged alleles. These mechanistic studies must be embedded within the integrative frameworks now defining the field: the bone–muscle axis [R57], the muscle–brain axis linking sarcopenia to cognitive decline [R58], and the muscle–tumor axis in breast cancer [R56]. Strikingly, none of these recent comprehensive syntheses names THBS4, which we view less as evidence of absence than as evidence that the protein has not yet been interrogated with the necessary tools in these contexts.

Finally, the field must explicitly engage with contemporary theories of ageing [R53, R59] to decide whether THBS4 should be targeted as a cause or consequence of sarcopenia. Optimisation frameworks predict that ageing-related phenotypes reflect evolutionary trade-offs rather than accumulated damage [R53], implying that boosting THBS4 in older adults could in principle restore a youthful program but might also override adaptive constraints, whereas mechanistic theories offer no specific prediction [R59]. Critically, what the existing data do NOT show is that circulating THBS4 decline causally precedes sarcopenia in humans; the correlative evidence [R47, R49] cannot distinguish driver from passenger, and the literature already contains discordant phenotypes—protection in dystrophin- and δ-sarcoglycan-deficient muscle [R2, R16] versus no effect in β-sarcoglycan- and laminin α2-deficient muscle [R24]—that underscore the context-dependence of any single model. Without isoform-resolved quantitation of circulating THBS4 paired with tissue-specific conditional genetics, the field will continue to generate context-dependent phenotypes without an actionable framework to guide therapeutic targeting, and the "rejuvenating factor" hypothesis will remain a seductive but untested narrative.
